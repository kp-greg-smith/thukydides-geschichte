Thucydides: The Peloponnesian War

Book One

Translation principles

Translate faithfully and readably, not word for word, and directly from the Ancient Greek. Hobbes, Crawley, Jowett and other translations may only help with understanding; they are neither the source text nor a translation to be taken over.

  1. A stable equivalent for each meaning. Do not force one word for every occurrence. αἰτία may mean accusation, cause, blame, or responsibility; a causal rendering is not excluded where the Greek sense requires it.
  2. Nothing added, nothing exaggerated. No explanations, imagery, claims, or emphasis absent from the Greek.
  3. Nothing softened. The force of harsh statements and of political vocabulary is preserved; δουλεία remains slavery in that sense.
  4. Notes for genuine uncertainty. Brief notes for textual uncertainty, genuine interpretive questions, or a significant change in a key term’s sense. Notes are not limited to damaged or corrupt text.
  5. Ambiguity preserved. Where the Greek allows more than one reading, that openness is kept where possible; unavoidable choices are explained in a brief note.
  6. Extra care with speeches and indirect speech. Speaker, addressee, argument, reported viewpoint, conditions, negation, modality, and temporal relations are preserved. Reported claims do not become the narrator’s assertions.

Chapter 1

§1 Thucydides the Athenian wrote the war of the Peloponnesians and the Athenians, how they fought it against one another, having begun at the very moment when it was breaking out, and in the expectation that it would prove great and the most noteworthy of all that had gone before; this he inferred from the fact that the two sides entered it at the height of their strength with the fullest preparations, and from seeing that the rest of the Greek world was ranging itself with one or the other of them, some immediately, and some in intention.

§2 For this was the greatest movement yet to come upon the Greeks, and upon a part of the barbarians as well — indeed, so to say, it reached the greatest part of mankind.

§3 For the events before these, and those more remote still, cannot be discovered with certainty because of the great lapse of time; but from such proofs as, on examining the matter as far as possible, I find myself able to trust, I judge that they were not of great account, either in respect of the wars or in anything else.

Chapter 2

§1 For the land now called Hellas appears not to have been securely inhabited from of old; on the contrary, migrations took place in earlier times, and each people readily left its own land, being always forced out by others more numerous.

§2 For commerce did not exist, and people did not mix with one another without fear, either by land or by sea; each cultivated from their own land only so much as sufficed to live on, and, having no surplus of resources, they planted no land either — since it was uncertain when someone might invade and carry it off, with their towns at the same time unfortified — while they believed that they could everywhere command the food necessary for each day. They therefore moved away without difficulty, and for this reason they had no strength either in the size of their cities or in any of their other preparations.

§3 The best land, above all, always saw changes of inhabitants: the land now called Thessaly, and Boeotia, and most of the Peloponnese except Arcadia, and, of the rest, whatever regions were best.

§4 For because of the excellence of the land, greater powers arose in certain places, and their growth produced civil wars through which they themselves were destroyed; and at the same time they were more plotted against by peoples of another stock.

§5 Attica, at least, thanks to the thinness of its soil, was for the longest time free from civil war, and was inhabited by the same people always.

§6 And this is by no means the least example in support of the argument: that the other parts of Greece did not grow in the same way because of the migrations. For the most powerful men driven out of the rest of Hellas by war or civil war withdrew to the Athenians as to a place of safety, and, becoming citizens at once, they made the city from early times still greater in the number of its people, so that later, when Attica was no longer sufficient for them, they sent out colonies to Ionia.

Chapter 3

§1 And the following also shows me, by no means least of all, the weakness of the men of old: before the Trojan war Hellas appears to have accomplished nothing in common beforehand;

§2 it seems to me, too, that the whole of Hellas did not yet bear this name, and that before Hellen, the son of Deucalion, this appellation was not even in existence; but that the various peoples, and the Pelasgian above all, mostly took their names each from its own stock; and that when Hellen and his sons had grown strong in Phthiotis, and men were inviting them into the other cities for the help they brought, the people of each city, through their association with them, came rather to be called Hellenes — yet the name was not able, for a long time, to prevail among all.

§3 Homer proves this best of all: born long after the Trojan war, he nowhere calls them all by this name; he calls none Hellenes but the followers of Achilles from Phthiotis, who were also the first Hellenes, while in his verses he invokes them as Danaans, Argives and Achaeans. Nor yet does he use the word barbarians anywhere, because, as it seems to me, the Hellenes were not yet marked off under one name standing in opposition.

§4 The Hellenes, then, in their several groups — as many, city by city, as understood one another, and afterwards, when they had all come to be so called — accomplished nothing in common before the Trojan war, because of weakness and their lack of intercourse with one another. Even this expedition they came together for only once they were already making greater use of the sea.

Chapter 4

§1 For Minos was the earliest of all whom we know of by report to have acquired a fleet; and he controlled most of the sea now called Greek, ruled the islands of the Cyclades, and was the first founder of most of them; having driven out the Carians, he installed his own sons as their governors. And, as was likely, he cleared piracy from the sea as far as he was able, so that his revenues should come in to him more abundantly.

Chapter 5

§1 For the Greeks of old and the barbarians whose homes lay along the mainland coast, and those who held the islands, took to piracy as soon as they began to make more frequent crossings in ships against one another; and their leaders were men of no mean power, who pursued it for the sake of their own gain and to get food for the weak; they would fall upon cities that were without walls and were inhabited in the manner of villages, and would plunder them, and from this they got most of their livelihood — the trade not yet carrying any shame with it, but rather a certain glory.

§2 Some of the mainland peoples make this plain even today, among whom it is a distinction to follow the practice; and the poets of old make it plain too, who everywhere alike, in the questions they put to those who come sailing in, ask whether they are pirates — since those whom they question did not disown the occupation, nor did those who cared to know cast it as a reproach.

§3 They plundered one another on the mainland as well; and many parts of Greece are still inhabited in the old manner, among the Ozolian Locrians and the Aetolians and the Acarnanians and the mainland in that region. The custom of bearing weapons has remained among these mainland peoples ever since the old brigandage.

Chapter 6

§1 For the whole of Hellas once went armed, because their settlements were without fortification and their approaches upon one another were not safe; and they made living under arms their accustomed manner of life, just like the barbarians.

§2 And these are a sign — that Hellas is still inhabited in this way — of ways of life that were once the same for all.

§3 The Athenians were the first, then, to lay aside their arms, and, relaxing their manner of life, to pass over into one of greater softness; and the elder men among their well-to-do, from their luxurious habits, ceased only a short time ago to wear linen chitons and to fasten up the hair of their heads in a knot with golden cicadas — a dress which, out of kinship, long held its ground among the elders of Ionia as well.

§4 And the Lacedaemonians were the first to use a modest dress such as now prevails; and in the rest of their life among the many, those among them who had the larger possessions came to live most nearly on a level with the common sort.

§5 They were the first, too, to strip and, undressing in the open, to rub themselves with oil when they exercised. In former times even in the Olympic games athletes used to compete wearing loincloths about their private parts, and it is not many years since that practice ceased. Even now among certain barbarians, and especially the peoples of Asia, prizes are offered for boxing and wrestling, and the contestants compete wearing loincloths.

§6 And many other things one could point to as evidence that the Greeks of old lived in much the same way as the barbarians of today.

Chapter 7

§1 Of the cities, those that were founded most recently, when voyaging was already easier, had a greater surplus of resources, and they were built with walls right upon the coasts and took possession of the isthmuses, for the sake of commerce and of the strength each would have over its neighbours; the older cities, on the other hand, were founded at a greater distance from the sea, because piracy long prevailed — those on the islands and those on the mainlands alike (for they plundered one another, and among the rest all who, being no seafarers, dwelt down below) — and even now they are still seated up-country. [Note: the transmitted ἀνῳκισμένοι is disputed; taken here as “still seated up-country”, though some read “not yet consolidated into a single town”.]

Chapter 8

§1 The islanders were pirates no less, being Carians and Phoenicians; for it was these who settled most of the islands. And there is testimony of it: when the Athenians purified Delos in the course of this war and the graves of all who had died in the island were taken up, more than half proved to be Carians, as was recognized both from the style of the arms buried together with them and from the manner of burial, which is the same as that in which they are buried even now.

§2 With the fleet of Minos established, voyaging between one another's lands became safer (for the lawless men of the islands were driven out by him — this being the very time at which he was settling most of the islands);

§3 and the peoples living by the sea, now that they were acquiring their wealth with more security, dwelt more firmly, and some of them even surrounded themselves with walls, since they were growing richer than they had been; for in their pursuit of gain the weaker were enduring the slavery of the stronger, and the stronger, holding a surplus of resources, made the lesser cities their subjects.

§4 It was thus, already in this condition of greater advancement, that at a later time they made their expedition against Troy.

Chapter 9

§1 Agamemnon, it seems to me, was pre-eminent among the men of that time in power, and it was not so much because he had the suitors of Helen bound by the oaths of Tyndareus that he raised his armament.

§2 The Peloponnesians, too, who have received the most accurate accounts in the tradition of those before them, say that Pelops was the first to acquire power, thanks to the abundance of money which he brought with him from Asia to men in want, and that, despite being an immigrant, he yet won the land's being named after him; and that the power was afterwards increased still further among his descendants: Eurystheus had been killed in Attica by the Heraclids, and Atreus was his mother's brother; Eurystheus, when he marched out on campaign, had entrusted Mycenae and his rule to Atreus as a kinsman — Atreus happened at the time to be living in exile from his father because of the death of Chrysippus — and once Eurystheus did not come back, Atreus, with the consent of the Mycenaeans too, who feared the Heraclids, took over the throne, being a man who seemed powerful and had courted the favour of the mass of the Mycenaeans and of all whom Eurystheus had ruled; and the Pelopids thus came to rank above the Perseids.

§3 These things, in my opinion, Agamemnon inherited; and being at the same time superior to the rest in naval power, he assembled the expedition not so much through goodwill as through fear.

§4 For he appears to have come in person with the largest fleet, and to have furnished ships to the Arcadians — a point which Homer has made clear, if he suffices as proof for anyone. And in the passage about the sceptre, with its handing down, he says that he “was lord of many islands and of all Argos”. Being a mainland man, then, he would not have held power over islands beyond those near his own coast (and these would not be many), unless he had also possessed some naval force. And one must estimate from this expedition too what the things before it were like.

Chapter 10

§1 The fact that Mycenae was small — or rather, that any town of that day now seems of no account — would be no sound sign on which to disbelieve that the armament was as great as the poets have described and as report has it.

§2 If the city of the Lacedaemonians were left desolate, and only the temples and the foundations of its public buildings survived, I think that when a long time had passed there would be great disbelief in their power among those who came after, in view of the renown of the Lacedaemonians (and yet they occupy two fifths of the Peloponnese, and they lead the whole of it, and many allies besides outside it; even so, since the city has never been consolidated into one town, and has made no use of costly temples and public buildings, but has been settled village by village in the old manner of Hellas, it would appear inferior), while if the Athenians suffered the same, their power would be estimated, from the visible appearance of the city, at twice what it is.

§3 It is not reasonable, then, to disbelieve, nor to weigh the appearance of cities more than their power; one should rather think that that expedition was the greatest up to that time, falling short of those of our own day — if here too one must place any trust in Homer's poetry, which, as was natural for a poet, he adorned to magnify — yet even so the expedition appears inferior.

§4 For of the twelve hundred ships he has made the Boeotian ones carry a hundred and twenty men apiece, and those of Philoctetes fifty — indicating, as it seems to me, the largest and the smallest; of the rest, at all events, there is no mention of their size in the Catalogue of Ships. That the crews were all also fighting men, he has shown in the ships of Philoctetes, for he has made all the rowers archers. Nor, as for those conveyed as passengers, is it likely that many, over and above the kings and the men in the highest offices, sailed — especially since they were about to cross the open sea with military equipment, in ships not fitted with decks, but prepared in the old style, in more piratical fashion. [Note: περίνεως, the transmitted word here, is disputed; it is taken to mean the men brought across by sea.]

§5 Judging, then, by the mean between the largest and the smallest ships, not many men appear to have taken part, for a force dispatched from the whole of Hellas in common.

Chapter 11

§1 The cause was not so much a scarcity of men as a lack of resources. For want of provisions they brought with them a smaller army, one only as large as they hoped could sustain itself by waging war from that land; and when they had arrived they prevailed in a battle (this is clear, for otherwise they would not have fortified their camp with a wall), and yet they appear to have employed not even then their whole strength, but, for want of provisions, to have turned to the cultivation of the Chersonese and to piracy. Because of this, with the Greeks dispersed, the Trojans were all the more able to hold out against them by force for those ten years, matched against only the forces that were continually left on the spot.

§2 If, however, they had come provided with a surplus of food, and being all together had prosecuted the war continuously, without piracy and without farming, they would easily have taken Troy by mastering the enemy in battle — they who even when not gathered in full force, but with only the part of their army ever present, held their own — and, sitting down to a siege, they would have taken Troy in less time and with less toil.

§3 But through lack of resources the undertakings before these were feeble, and these very ones themselves, the most famous of the deeds of former times, are shown by the deeds to be inferior to their fame and to the account of them which now, through the poets, has got possession of men’s minds;

Chapter 12

§1 For even after the Trojan War Hellas was still changing its settlements and being resettled, so that, having no quiet, it did not grow.

§2 For the return of the Greeks from Ilium took place only after long delay, and occasioned many disturbances; civil wars, too, broke out in the cities for the most part, in consequence of which men, being driven out, founded cities.

§3 For the Boeotians of today, in the sixtieth year after the capture of Ilium, left Arne under pressure from the Thessalians and settled the land now called Boeotia but formerly named Cadmeis (part of them had also held allotments in this land at an earlier time, and it was from these that they marched against Ilium); the Dorians in the eightieth year took the Peloponnese with the Heraclids.

§4 Only after a long time, and with difficulty, did Hellas come to rest, and then, no longer shifting its abodes, it sent out colonies on a firm footing: the Athenians sent out the Ionians and most of the islanders as colonists, and the Peloponnesians most of Italy and Sicily, together with some districts of the rest of Hellas. All these were founded after the Trojan War.

Chapter 13

§1 As Hellas grew more powerful and came to acquire resources still more than before, tyrannies were for the most part set up in the cities, as revenues increased (previously there had been hereditary kingships, based on specified prerogatives), and Hellas began to be furnished with a fleet and to cling more to the sea.

§2 The Corinthians are said to have been the first to handle the business of ships in the manner nearest to that of today, and it is said that the first triremes of Hellas were built at Corinth.

§3 And Ameinocles, a Corinthian shipwright, appears to have built four ships for the Samians; and it is about three hundred years, to the end of this war, since Ameinocles came to the Samians.

§4 And the oldest naval battle that we know of took place between Corinthians and Corcyraeans; and in this case too it is about two hundred and sixty years to the same point of time.

§5 For the Corinthians, whose city was on the Isthmus, had always at all times held a market, because the Greeks of old, those within the Peloponnese and those outside it, associated with one another for the most part by land rather than by sea, and through the Corinthians’ territory; they were powerful in resources, as is shown also by the ancient poets, who named the place “wealthy”; and when the Greeks came to sail more, the Corinthians acquired ships and put down piracy, and by furnishing the market they made their city powerful in both ways through the revenues of its resources.

§6 And much later the Ionians acquired a fleet, in the reign of Cyrus, the first king of the Persians, and of Cambyses his son; and fighting Cyrus they were masters for a time of their own sea. And Polycrates, tyrant of Samos, who in the reign of Cambyses owed his strength to his fleet, made certain others of the islands subject to himself, and, having taken Rhenea, dedicated it to the Delian Apollo. And the Phocaeans, in the course of founding Massalia, defeated the Carthaginians in a naval battle;

Chapter 14

§1 For these were the mightiest of the fleets. And yet these too, it appears, came into being many generations after the Trojan War, using but few triremes, and still equipped, like those, with fifty-oared ships and long vessels.

§2 And a little before the Median war and the death of Darius, who reigned over the Persians after Cambyses, triremes came to abound in the hands of the tyrants in Sicily and of the Corcyraeans; for these were the last considerable fleets established in Hellas before the expedition of Xerxes.

§3 For Aeginetans and Athenians, and any others that there were, possessed but a few ships, and most of these were fifty-oared; and it was only at a late date that Themistocles persuaded the Athenians, who were at war with the Aeginetans and had at the same time the barbarian to expect, to build the ships with which they also fought at sea; and even these ships did not yet have decks along their whole length.

Chapter 15

§1 The Greek fleets, then, both the ancient ones and those that arose later, were of this character. Yet none the less those who devoted themselves to them acquired no inconsiderable strength, from the revenues of their resources and from rule over others; for by sailing against the islands they subdued them, especially such as had not land sufficient.

§2 But by land no war arose from which any power came into being; all the wars, such as there were, were with neighbouring peoples, in each case their own; and the Greeks did not send out expeditions abroad, far from their own land, for the overthrow of others. For they had not been drawn together as subjects under the greatest cities; nor did they for their part conduct expeditions in common on a footing of equality; but rather the neighbouring peoples fought against one another, each on its own.

§3 And above all, over the war which took place long ago between the Chalcidians and the Eretrians, the rest of Hellas was divided into alliance with either party.

Chapter 16

§1 Obstacles to growth arose, moreover, for others also in various places; and for the Ionians, when their affairs had advanced far, there came Cyrus and the Persian kingdom, which, having overthrown Croesus, attacked everything on this side of the river Halys towards the sea, and enslaved the cities on the mainland; and Darius later, being master through the Phoenicians’ fleet, reduced the islands as well.

Chapter 17

§1 And the tyrants, as many as there were in the Greek cities, looked only to what was their own — to their persons and to the increase of their own households — and, as much as they were able, governed the cities in the way that was safest for themselves; and nothing worthy of note was accomplished by them, except perhaps some act of each against his own neighbours; for those in Sicily advanced furthest in power. Thus for a long time, in every way, Hellas was kept back from accomplishing anything notable in common, and, city by city, was the more timid.

Chapter 18

§1 But when the tyrants of the Athenians and those of the rest of Hellas — a Hellas that had long before come under tyrannies — had been put down by the Lacedaemonians, most of them and the last, apart from those in Sicily (for Lacedaemon, after the Dorians who now inhabit her had settled her, though in civil strife for the longest time that we know of, had nevertheless been well governed from the very earliest times and never had a tyrant; for it is about four hundred years, and a little more, to the end of this war since the Lacedaemonians have used the same constitution, and owing to this they were powerful and likewise settled affairs in the other cities), only a few years after the removal of the tyrants from Hellas came also the battle of Marathon between the Medes and the Athenians. [Note: the transmitted “ἐπὶ πολὺ καὶ πρὶν τυραννευθείσης” is textually disordered; taken as “a Hellas that had long before come under tyrannies”.]

§2 And in the tenth year after it the barbarian came again with his great armament against Hellas, to enslave it. And when great danger hung over them, the Lacedaemonians took the lead of the Greeks who had shared in the war, being pre-eminent in power, and the Athenians, as the Medes came on, resolved to abandon their city, and, moving their households on board the ships and embarking, became a seafaring people. And when they had jointly repelled the barbarian, not much later the Greeks who had revolted from the King and those who had shared in the war were divided between Athenians and Lacedaemonians; for in power these stood out the greatest — the one side was strong by land, the other by sea.

§3 And the fellowship in arms lasted but a short time; then, when they had quarrelled, Lacedaemonians and Athenians fought against each other, each with his allies; and whenever others of the Greeks anywhere took opposite sides, against these the two were now marching. So from the Median war down to this present war, at one time making truce, at another fighting — against one another, or against their own allies as they revolted — they made good preparations for war and grew more experienced, taking their practice in the midst of dangers.

Chapter 19

§1 The Lacedaemonians, then, led their allies without holding them tributary, attending to them only as far as was convenient to their own oligarchy, with an eye to how they were to be governed; the Athenians, however, took over in course of time the ships of the cities — those of the Chians and Lesbians excepted — and set money for them all to pay. And for this war the preparations that were their own had become greater than at the time when they flourished at their best, while the alliance was still unimpaired.

Chapter 20

§1 The ancient matters, then, I found to be of this character — difficult to believe on the evidence of any continuous proof. Men accept the hearsay of events before their time from one another without scrutiny, all alike, even when it concerns their own country.

§2 The mass of the Athenians, for example, think that Hipparchus, being tyrant, died at the hands of Harmodius and Aristogiton, and they do not know that Hippias, as the eldest, was ruler of the sons of Pisistratus, and that Hipparchus and Thessalus were his brothers; nor that Harmodius and Aristogiton, having formed some suspicion on that day, and at once learning from those who shared the plot with them that Hippias had been informed, held off from him as being forewarned, but, wishing, before they were arrested, to do some deed and run its risk, fell in with Hipparchus near the place called the Leocorium, while he was arranging the Panathenaic procession, and killed him.

§3 And many other things besides, which still exist now and are not lost to memory through time, the rest of the Greeks wrongly suppose — such as that each of the kings of the Lacedaemonians is admitted not with a single vote, but with two, and that there exists among them the Pitanate company, which never existed at all. So little trouble do the many take over the search for truth, and they turn rather to what lies ready to hand.

Chapter 21

§1 From the proofs I have given, all the same, a man who judged such things as I have gone through to be so above all would not be wrong — putting faith neither in the poets, who have hymned them, adorning them towards the greater, nor in the logographers, who have composed them with an eye rather to what would win the hearing than to what was more true, these matters being beyond scrutiny and having for the most part won their way, under the influence of time, into the mythical without winning belief; and judging that they have been sufficiently established, from the most conspicuous signs, to be ancient.

§2 And this war, although men always judge the war in which they happen to be engaged to be the greatest, and once they have ceased rather admire the things of old, will none the less make it plain, to those who examine the deeds themselves, that it became greater than they.

Chapter 22

§1 As for what was said in speech by the several parties, either when they were about to make war or when they were already in it, it was difficult to carry in memory the strict accuracy of what was said, both for me, in the case of what I myself heard, and for those who reported it to me from various quarters. But as it seemed to me each would have said what was most to the purpose about the matters ever present to the occasion, keeping as close as possible to the entire sense of what was truly said, so it has been set down.

§2 As for the deeds done in the war, I did not think it right to write them down from whatever enquiry happened to come my way, nor as they seemed to me, but from the actions at which I was present myself, and, and from my enquiries of others, with as much accuracy as was possible, going over each detail.

§3 And the truth was discovered only with effort, because those present at the several actions did not say the same things about the same events, but each according as he had partiality or memory for one side or the other.

§4 And for the hearing, perhaps, the absence of the mythical in these things will prove less delightful; but it will be enough if those who wish to examine both the clear fact of what has happened and of what will some day, according to the human condition, again be such and the like, judge it useful; and it is composed rather as a possession for ever than as a competition-piece to be heard for the moment.

Chapter 23

§1 Of the deeds of former times the greatest was that of the Median war, and yet even this one received its decision quickly, in two naval battles and two on land; this war, by contrast, was protracted to a great length, and such sufferings befell Hellas in the course of it as had occurred in no other equal time.

§2 For neither were so many cities captured and laid waste — some by the barbarians, others by their own people warring against one another, and there are some, too, that upon being taken exchanged their inhabitants — nor were there so many exiles of men and so much slaughter, the one from the war itself, the other from the civil strife.

§3 And things formerly told by hearsay, and more rarely confirmed in fact, were no longer incredible: earthquakes, which at one and the same time affected the greatest extent of land and were themselves the most violent; eclipses of the sun, which occurred more frequently than those remembered from the time before; droughts, great in some places, and famines arising from them besides; and the pestilential disease, which did by no means the least harm and destroyed a part. For all these things came upon them at the same time together with this war.

§4 The Athenians and the Peloponnesians began it by breaking the thirty years' truce which had been made between them after the capture of Euboea.

§5 Why they broke it, I have written down first — the causes and the differences — so that no one may ever seek from what source so great a war settled upon the Greeks.

§6 For the truest ground, though least openly stated in words, I judge to be that the Athenians were becoming great and were causing fear in the Lacedaemonians, and so compelled them to make war; while the causes alleged in the open by either side, from which they broke the truce and entered upon the war, were the following.

Chapter 24

§1 Epidamnus is a city on the right as one sails into the Ionian Gulf; and the Taulantii, a barbarian people of Illyria, dwell round about it.

§2 This city the Corcyraeans sent out as a colony; and the founder of it was Phalius son of Eratocleides, a Corinthian, of the family of the Heraclids, summoned from the mother city according to the ancient custom; and there joined in the settlement some Corinthians also, and men of the rest of the Dorian race.

§3 With the advance of time the power of the Epidamnians became great and populous.

§4 Then, having been in civil war among themselves for many years, as it is said, they came to ruin through some war with the neighbouring barbarians, and were stripped of much of their power.

§5 Most lately, before this present war, the commons of the city drove out the powerful, and these, coming upon them with the barbarians, plundered those who were in the city, both by land and by sea.

§6 The Epidamnians who were in the city, being hard pressed, sent envoys to Corcyra as to their mother city, begging them not to look on while they were being destroyed, but to reconcile the exiles to them and to put an end to the war against the barbarians.

§7 These requests they made, sitting as suppliants in the Heraeum; and the Corcyraeans did not accept the supplication, but sent them away with nothing accomplished.

Chapter 25

§1 When the Epidamnians perceived that no redress was coming to them from Corcyra, being at a loss they resolved to take up the course that lay before them, and sending to Delphi they asked the god whether they should hand the city over to the Corinthians as to its founders, and should try to obtain some redress from them. And the god answered them: to hand it over, and to take them as their leaders.

§2 So the Epidamnians came to Corinth and, in obedience to the oracle, delivered the colony over, declaring the founder of their city to be a man of Corinth and making known the oracle's answer, and begging the Corinthians not to look on while they were being destroyed, but to come to their aid.

§3 The Corinthians undertook the redress as a matter of right, holding the colony to be theirs no less than the Corcyraeans', and at the same time also out of hatred for the Corcyraeans, because these, being settlers of theirs, paid them no regard.

§4 For they granted the Corinthians neither the customary prerogatives at the common festivals, nor precedence in the sacred rites to a man of Corinth, as the other colonies did, but despised them, while in resources they stood at that time like the richest of the Greeks, and in their preparations for war were the stronger; and there were times when they were exalted, in the matter of their fleet, to the point of being far ahead, resting on the Phaeacians' fame in things of the sea — they who had inhabited Corcyra before them (and herein they also equipped their fleet the further, and were not powerless; for they had one hundred and twenty triremes when they took up the war).

Chapter 26

§1 The Corinthians, having then complaints against them on all these counts, gladly sent the help to Epidamnus, inviting any who wished to go as a settler, and sending as garrison Ambraciots and Leucadians and men of their own.

§2 They went by land to Apollonia, a colony of the Corinthians, for fear that the Corcyraeans might prevent them from making the crossing by sea.

§3 When the Corcyraeans learned that the settlers and the garrison had arrived at Epidamnus, and that the colony had been handed over to the Corinthians, they were indignant; and sailing at once with twenty-five ships, and after that with another squadron, they bade the Epidamnians, under threat, receive back their exiles — for the Epidamnian exiles had come to Corcyra, showing the tombs and the kinship, and on this plea begging that they be brought back — and dismiss the garrison sent by the Corinthians and the settlers.

§4 The Epidamnians obeyed them in nothing; whereupon the Corcyraeans took the field against them with forty ships, taking the exiles with them in the intention of bringing them back, and enlisting the Illyrians besides.

§5 Taking up their position before the city, they gave notice that any Epidamnian who wished, and the foreigners, might depart unharmed; failing this, they would be dealt with as enemies. And when they were not persuaded, the Corcyraeans — the place is an isthmus — laid siege to the city.

Chapter 27

§1 But when there came to the Corinthians messengers with news that it was under siege, they set about preparing an expedition, and at the same time proclaimed a colony for Epidamnus, on the equal and like footing, open to any who wished to go; while if anyone did not choose to sail with them at that moment, but wished to have a share in the colony, he might remain, on paying down fifty Corinthian drachmas. And they who sailed were many, and they too who paid down the money.

§2 They asked the Megarians also to send ships to sail with them, in case they should be prevented by the Corcyraeans from sailing; and the Megarians prepared to sail in company with them with eight ships, and Pala of the Cephallenians with four. They asked the Epidaurians too, who furnished five; Hermione one, and the Troezenians two; the Leucadians ten, and the Ambraciots eight. From the Thebans and the Phliasians they asked money, and from the Eleians empty ships and money. Of the Corinthians' own there were being fitted out thirty ships and three thousand hoplites.

Chapter 28

§1 When the Corcyraeans learned of these preparations, they came to Corinth, bringing with them envoys of the Lacedaemonians and of the Sicyonians, whom they had secured, and required the Corinthians to withdraw from Epidamnus the garrison and the settlers, on the ground that they had no part in Epidamnus.

§2 If the Corinthians advanced any counter-claim, they were willing to submit the matter to trial, before whatever Peloponnesian cities both parties might agree upon; and whichever of the two should be adjudged to have the colony, that party was to keep it. They were willing also to refer it to the oracle at Delphi.

§3 War, however, they would not allow them to make; but they said that, if it came to that, they too would be compelled, since force was being used against them, to take as friends men whom they did not wish — others, that is, than those they had at present — and this rather for the sake of advantage.

§4 The Corinthians answered them that, if they would withdraw their ships and the barbarians from Epidamnus, they would then take counsel; before that, they said, it was not right that the one party should be under siege while they themselves sat in judgement.

§5 The Corcyraeans answered in turn that if the Corinthians also would withdraw the men in Epidamnus, they would do these things; and they declared themselves ready for both parties to remain where they were, and to make a truce until the trial should take place.

Chapter 29

§1 But the Corinthians complied with none of these things: when their ships were fully manned and their allies were present, they first sent on a herald to declare war against the Corcyraeans, and then put to sea with seventy-five ships and two thousand hoplites and sailed against Epidamnus, to make war in opposition to the Corcyraeans.

§2 Of the ships Aristeus son of Pellichus was in command, and Callicrates son of Callias, and Timanor son of Timanthes; of the land force Archetimus son of Eurytimus, and Isarchidas son of Isarchus.

§3 And when they came to Actium, in the territory of Anactorium, where the sanctuary of Apollo is, at the mouth of the Ambracian Gulf, the Corcyraeans sent on to them a herald in a small boat to say to ask them not to sail against them, and at the same time were manning their own ships, having secured the older vessels so as to make them fit for sailing and refitted the rest.

§4 When the herald came back bringing no peaceable answer from the Corinthians, and their own ships had been brought to full strength, eighty in number (for forty ships were besieging Epidamnus), the Corcyraeans put out against them, and, having formed their line, fought a battle by sea.

§5 And the Corcyraeans won by a great margin, and destroyed fifteen of the Corinthians' ships; and on that same day it befell them besides that their men besieging Epidamnus brought the city over by agreement, so that the new-comers should be sold, while the Corinthians should be held bound until some other decision should be taken.

Chapter 30

§1 After the sea-fight the Corcyraeans set up a trophy on Leukimme, the promontory of Corcyra, and the rest of the captives whom they had taken they killed, but the Corinthians they held in bonds.

§2 Later, when the Corinthians and their allies had withdrawn to their homes, having been defeated by sea, the Corcyraeans commanded all the sea about those parts, and, sailing against Leucas, the colony of the Corinthians, they ravaged its land, and they burned Cyllene, the harbour of the Eleians, because it had furnished the Corinthians with ships and money.

§3 For the greater part of the time after the battle they retained this command of the sea, and harried the allies of the Corinthians, sailing against them, until the Corinthians, as the summer was passing its course, sent out ships and an army — since their own allies were being worn down — and made their camp at Actium, and about Cheimerium in Thesprotis, for the guarding of Leucas and of the other cities as many as were friendly to them.

§4 The Corcyraeans for their part encamped opposite them at Leukimme, with ships and also with land forces; and neither side put out to sea against the other, but, sitting thus opposite one another for this summer, both alike went home, as winter was already coming on.
