Thucydides: The Peloponnesian War

Book One

Translation principles

Translate faithfully and readably, not word for word, and directly from the Ancient Greek. Hobbes, Crawley, Jowett and other translations may only help with understanding; they are neither the source text nor a translation to be taken over.

  1. A stable equivalent for each meaning. Do not force one word for every occurrence. αἰτία may mean accusation, cause, blame, or responsibility; a causal rendering is not excluded where the Greek sense requires it.
  2. Nothing added, nothing exaggerated. No explanations, imagery, claims, or emphasis absent from the Greek.
  3. Nothing softened. The force of harsh statements and of political vocabulary is preserved; δουλεία remains slavery in that sense.
  4. Notes for genuine uncertainty. Brief notes for textual uncertainty, genuine interpretive questions, or a significant change in a key term’s sense. Notes are not limited to damaged or corrupt text.
  5. Ambiguity preserved. Where the Greek allows more than one reading, that openness is kept where possible; unavoidable choices are explained in a brief note.
  6. Extra care with speeches and indirect speech. Speaker, addressee, argument, reported viewpoint, conditions, negation, modality, and temporal relations are preserved. Reported claims do not become the narrator’s assertions.

Chapter 1

§1 Thucydides of Athens wrote this account of the war between the Peloponnesians and the Athenians, describing how they fought one another. He began as soon as the war broke out, expecting it to be great and more noteworthy than any earlier war. His grounds were that both sides entered it at the height of their strength, fully prepared in every respect. He also saw the rest of the Greek world taking sides, some immediately and others intending to do so.

§2 This was the greatest upheaval the Greeks had experienced, and it affected some of the barbarians too: one could say that it extended to most of humanity.

§3 The events before this war, and those of the more distant past, could not be established with certainty because so much time had elapsed. But from the evidence I find credible after looking as far back as possible, I judge that they were not on a great scale, either in warfare or in other respects.

Chapter 2

§1 The land now called Greece evidently had no settled population in early times. There were migrations, and each people readily abandoned its land whenever a more numerous group forced it out.

§2 There was no trade, and people could not travel safely to one another by land or sea. Each worked their own land only enough to live on. They had no accumulated wealth and planted no permanent crops, since they never knew when an invader might take them, and their settlements had no walls. Believing they could obtain their daily food anywhere, they moved away without difficulty. As a result, they had neither large cities nor substantial resources of any other kind.

§3 The most fertile land saw the most frequent changes of inhabitants: what is now called Thessaly, Boeotia, most of the Peloponnese except Arcadia, and the best land elsewhere.

§4 The fertile soil enabled some to gain greater power, which provoked civil strife that ruined the communities. At the same time, these places were more exposed to designs against them by other peoples.

§5 Attica, for example, was free from civil strife from the earliest times because of its poor soil, and always had the same inhabitants.

§6 There is strong evidence for the argument that migrations prevented the rest of Greece from growing at the same rate. The most powerful of those driven out of other parts of Greece by war or civil strife took refuge in Athens as a place of safety. They became citizens and, from very early times, further increased the city’s population. Eventually Attica could no longer accommodate them, and the Athenians sent colonies to Ionia.

Chapter 3

§1 Another strong indication of the weakness of earlier times is that Greece apparently undertook nothing jointly before the Trojan War.

§2 I do not think the whole country even bore the name Hellas yet. Before Hellen, son of Deucalion, the name seems not to have existed at all. Instead, different peoples gave their own names to the regions they inhabited, most widely the Pelasgians. When Hellen and his sons grew powerful in Phthiotis and were invited to help other cities, these communities increasingly came to be called Hellenes through their contact with them. But it took a long time for the name to prevail among them all.

§3 Homer provides the clearest evidence. Although he lived long after the Trojan War, he never uses the name Hellenes for them all, only for Achilles’ followers from Phthiotis, who were the first Hellenes. For the Greeks collectively, his poems use the names Danaans, Argives and Achaeans. Nor does he use the name barbarians. In my view, this was because the Hellenes had not yet been distinguished by a single name in opposition to them.

§4 Those who came to be called Hellenes, first city by city as they came to understand one another’s language, and later as a whole, undertook nothing together before the Trojan War. Their weakness and lack of contact prevented it. Even that joint expedition became possible only when they had begun making greater use of the sea.

Chapter 4

§1 Minos is the earliest person we know of by report to have acquired a fleet. He controlled most of what is now the Greek sea, ruled the Cyclades and was the first to establish colonies on most of those islands. He drove out the Carians and installed his own sons as rulers. Presumably he also suppressed piracy at sea as far as he could, so that his revenues would reach him more readily.

Chapter 5

§1 In early times, the Greeks and the barbarians living on the mainland coasts and islands turned to piracy as they began to cross the sea more often to one another. They were led by men of considerable power who sought profit for themselves and food for the poor. They attacked and plundered unwalled towns whose inhabitants lived in scattered villages, and made most of their living in this way. Such activity carried no shame at the time; it brought a certain honour instead.

§2 Some mainland peoples still show this today: successful raiding is an honour among them. So do the early poets, in whose works people arriving by sea are regularly asked the same question: are they pirates? Those questioned did not consider the occupation beneath them, and those wanting to know meant no reproach.

§3 People also plundered one another on land. Many parts of Greece still live in the old way, including the Ozolian Locrians, the Aetolians, the Acarnanians and the neighbouring mainland. The habit of carrying weapons has survived among these mainland peoples from the old days of raiding.

Chapter 6

§1 All Greece once went armed, because settlements were unfortified and travel between them was unsafe. People habitually carried weapons in daily life, as the barbarians do.

§2 The parts of Greece that still live this way are evidence that these customs were once shared by everyone.

§3 The Athenians were among the first to lay down their weapons and adopt a more relaxed, luxurious way of life. Only recently did the older men among the wealthy abandon the linen tunics and hair tied in a knot with golden cicadas that reflected their taste for luxury. Because of their kinship with the Athenians, older Ionians also kept this style of dress for a long time.

§4 The Lacedaemonians were the first to adopt modest clothing of the kind worn today. In other respects too, their richer citizens came closest to sharing the way of life of ordinary people.

§5 They were also the first to strip naked in public and rub themselves with oil when exercising. In earlier times, even at the Olympic Games, athletes competed with loincloths covering their genitals; this practice ended only a few years ago. Even now, some barbarian peoples, especially in Asia, offer prizes for boxing and wrestling, and the contestants compete in loincloths.

§6 Many other examples could be given to show that the Greeks of old lived much as the barbarians do today.

Chapter 7

§1 Cities founded more recently, when navigation had become easier and more resources were available, were built with walls right on the coast. Their inhabitants also occupied the isthmuses, for trade and to strengthen their position against their neighbours. Older cities, both on the islands and on the mainland, had been built further from the sea because piracy had long prevailed. For people plundered one another, as well as coastal inhabitants who were not seafarers. These older settlements remain inland to this day.

Chapter 8

§1 The islanders were no less given to piracy. They were Carians and Phoenicians, who had settled most of the islands. There is evidence of this: when the Athenians purified Delos during this war and dug up all the graves on the island, more than half the dead proved to be Carians. They were identified by the type of weapons buried with them and by the burial customs which the Carians still follow today.

§2 Once Minos had established his fleet, sailing between communities became safer. He had driven the criminals out of the islands when he settled most of them.

§3 The people along the coast now accumulated more wealth and lived in greater security. Some, growing richer than before, even surrounded their settlements with walls. In pursuit of profit, the weaker accepted slavery under the stronger, while the more powerful used their accumulated wealth to make the smaller cities their subjects.

§4 After developing further along these lines, they eventually launched the expedition against Troy.

Chapter 9

§1 In my view, Agamemnon assembled the expedition because he surpassed his contemporaries in power, rather than simply because he led Helen’s suitors, who were bound by their oaths to Tyndareus.

§2 The Peloponnesians who have inherited the most reliable accounts from earlier generations also tell the following story. Pelops first gained power through the great wealth he brought from Asia to an impoverished people. Although a foreigner, he gave his name to the country. Later his descendants became still more powerful. Eurystheus was killed in Attica by the descendants of Heracles. Before leaving on campaign, he had entrusted Mycenae and his rule to his mother’s brother Atreus, because of their kinship. Atreus was then in exile from his father because of Chrysippus’s death. When Eurystheus did not return, Atreus took over the kingship of Mycenae and all the other lands Eurystheus had ruled. The Mycenaeans agreed: they feared the descendants of Heracles, regarded Atreus as powerful, and he had won popular favour. In this way the descendants of Pelops became more powerful than those of Perseus.

§3 Agamemnon, I think, inherited this power and also had a stronger fleet than the others. He assembled and led the expedition through fear more than goodwill.

§4 He evidently arrived with the most ships himself and also supplied ships to the Arcadians. Homer makes this clear, if one accepts him as sufficient evidence. In his account of how the sceptre was handed down, he also says that Agamemnon ruled “many islands and all Argos”. As a mainland ruler, he could not have controlled islands beyond those nearby, which would not have been many, unless he also possessed a fleet. This expedition should also guide our judgement of those that preceded it.

Chapter 10

§1 Mycenae was small, and a town from those days may now seem insignificant. These would be unreliable grounds for doubting that the expedition was as large as the poets and tradition say.

§2 Suppose the city of the Lacedaemonians were abandoned, leaving only its temples and the foundations of its buildings. I think that, after a long time had passed, later generations would find it hard to believe that its power had matched its fame. Yet the Lacedaemonians occupy two fifths of the Peloponnese and lead the whole peninsula, as well as many allies outside it. Their city would still seem less powerful than it was, because it is not a single compact settlement with expensive temples and buildings: it consists of villages in the old Greek manner. If Athens suffered the same fate, however, its visible remains would lead people to estimate its power at twice its true strength.

§3 There is therefore no reason for disbelief, or for judging cities by their appearance rather than their power. We should regard the Trojan expedition as the greatest of its time, but smaller than those of today, if we can trust Homer’s poetry on this point too. As a poet, he probably embellished it to make it seem greater, yet even his account shows it to have been smaller.

§4 Of the twelve hundred ships, Homer gives the Boeotian ones a hundred and twenty men each, and those of Philoctetes fifty. These seem to me to represent the largest and smallest ships; at any rate, he mentions no other sizes in the Catalogue of Ships. He shows, in his account of Philoctetes' ships, that all the men both rowed and fought, since he describes all the rowers as archers. Apart from the kings and the highest-ranking men, many additional passengers are unlikely to have sailed with them. They were, after all, preparing to cross the open sea with military equipment, and their ships were not fully decked, but equipped in the older style, more like pirate vessels.

§5 Taking an average between the largest and smallest ships, the number of men who sailed appears small for an expedition sent jointly by all Greece.

Chapter 11

§1 The reason was less a shortage of men than a lack of money. Difficulty in obtaining provisions limited the army they brought to the number they hoped could live off the country while fighting. They won a battle on arrival; otherwise they could not have fortified their camp. Yet even then they evidently did not employ their full strength. Lack of food made them turn to farming in the Chersonese and to raiding. Their forces were dispersed, which helped the Trojans resist them for ten years: the Trojans were a match for whichever troops remained at any given time.

§2 If they had arrived with ample provisions and kept their army together, fighting continuously instead of raiding and farming, they would easily have defeated the Trojans in battle and taken the city. Even with only part of their force present at any time, they held their own. By maintaining a siege, they could have captured Troy sooner and with less effort.

§3 Lack of money thus limited the earlier undertakings. Even the events of the Trojan War, the most famous of ancient times, prove on examination to have been less substantial than their reputation and the account of them now established by the poets.

Chapter 12

§1 Even after the Trojan War, populations in Greece were still moving and resettling. It lacked the stability needed to grow.

§2 The Greeks’ long-delayed return from Troy caused many disturbances. Civil strife broke out widely in the cities, and those expelled went on to found new cities.

§3 In the sixtieth year after the capture of Troy, the people who are now the Boeotians were driven from Arne by the Thessalians. They settled what is now Boeotia, previously called the land of Cadmus. Some of them had already lived there, and it was this group that had joined the expedition against Troy. In the eightieth year, the Dorians and the descendants of Heracles took possession of the Peloponnese.

§4 Only after a long and difficult period did Greece become firmly settled and, with its people no longer being driven out, send out colonies. The Athenians settled Ionia and most of the islands. The Peloponnesians settled most of Italy and Sicily, as well as certain places elsewhere in Greece. All these settlements were founded after the Trojan War.

Chapter 13

§1 As Greece grew more powerful and accumulated more wealth, rising revenues brought tyrannies to many cities. Previously there had been hereditary kingships with defined prerogatives. The Greeks equipped fleets and turned increasingly to the sea.

§2 The Corinthians are said to have been the first to develop shipbuilding in a way close to the practice of our own day. The first triremes in Greece are also said to have been built at Corinth.

§3 The Corinthian shipbuilder Ameinocles evidently built four ships for the Samians. His arrival at Samos was about three hundred years before the end of this war.

§4 The earliest naval battle we know of was fought between the Corinthians and the Corcyraeans, about two hundred and sixty years before that same date.

§5 Because their city stood on the Isthmus, the Corinthians had always possessed a trading centre. In early times the Greeks inside and outside the Peloponnese travelled to one another mostly by land, through Corinthian territory, rather than by sea. This made the Corinthians financially powerful, as the early poets also attest when they call the place “wealthy”. When the Greeks began sailing more, the Corinthians acquired ships and suppressed piracy. By providing a trading centre for both land and sea, they made their city powerful through its revenues.

§6 Later, the Ionians too acquired a large fleet, in the reigns of Cyrus, the first Persian king, and his son Cambyses. While fighting Cyrus, they controlled their own stretch of sea for a time. Polycrates, tyrant of Samos in the reign of Cambyses, used his naval power to subject various islands to his rule. He also captured Rhenea and dedicated it to Apollo of Delos. The Phocaeans, while founding Massalia, defeated the Carthaginians at sea.

Chapter 14

§1 These were the most powerful fleets. Yet even they, formed many generations after the Trojan War, evidently used few triremes. They were still equipped mainly with fifty-oared ships and long vessels, like the earlier fleets.

§2 Only shortly before the Median Wars and the death of Darius, who ruled Persia after Cambyses, did the tyrants in Sicily and the Corcyraeans acquire large numbers of triremes. These were the last substantial fleets established in Greece before Xerxes’ expedition.

§3 The Aeginetans, Athenians and any others had few ships, mostly fifty-oared vessels. Only at a late stage, while the Athenians were at war with Aegina and expecting the barbarian invasion, did Themistocles persuade them to build the ships in which they fought their sea battles. Even these still lacked decks along their full length.

Chapter 15

§1 Such were the Greek fleets, both earlier and later. Even so, those who developed them gained considerable power through revenue and rule over others. They sailed against the islands and conquered them; those with insufficient land of their own were particularly active in doing so.

§2 No war on land produced a growth of power. Such wars as occurred were fought between neighbours. The Greeks did not go on campaigns far from their own country to conquer others. They had neither united as subjects of the greatest cities nor joined forces as equals in common expeditions. Neighbouring communities generally fought one another on their own.

§3 The ancient war between Chalcis and Eretria was the clearest case in which the rest of Greece also divided into alliances with the two sides.

Chapter 16

§1 Different obstacles to growth arose in different places. Just as the Ionians were making great progress, Cyrus and the Persian empire attacked them, after overthrowing Croesus and conquering all the land between the river Halys and the sea. The mainland cities were enslaved. Later Darius, using the Phoenician fleet to establish his supremacy, enslaved the islands too.

Chapter 17

§1 The tyrants in the Greek cities looked only to their own interests: their personal safety and the prosperity of their households. They governed as cautiously as possible, and achieved nothing worth recording beyond occasional attacks by each on his own neighbours. Those in Sicily reached the greatest power. Thus Greece was held back on every side for a long time, unable to achieve anything notable together and lacking boldness in its individual cities.

Chapter 18

§1 The Lacedaemonians overthrew the tyrants of Athens and most of the last tyrants elsewhere in Greece, where tyranny had long been widespread, except those in Sicily. After the Dorians who now inhabit Lacedaemon settled there, the city endured civil war for the longest period known to us. Nevertheless, it had enjoyed good government from very early times and had always been free of tyrants. By the end of this war, the Lacedaemonians had lived under the same constitution for a little over four hundred years. The power this gave them also enabled them to settle affairs in other cities. Only a few years after the overthrow of the tyrants in Greece, the battle of Marathon took place between the Medes and the Athenians.

§2 In the tenth year after Marathon, the barbarian returned with a great force to enslave Greece. Faced with this enormous danger, the Greeks who fought together were led by the Lacedaemonians, who surpassed the others in power. As the Medes advanced, the Athenians decided to abandon their city, evacuated their possessions, boarded their ships and became a naval power. Together the Greeks drove back the barbarian. Soon afterwards, both those who had revolted from the King and those who had fought in the common war divided between Athens and Lacedaemon. These two powers clearly stood out: the Lacedaemonians were strong on land, the Athenians at sea.

§3 Their military alliance lasted only a short time. The Lacedaemonians and Athenians then quarrelled and fought one another with their respective allies. Whenever other Greeks fell into disputes, they now joined one or the other side. From the Median Wars until this war, there were periods of truce and periods of fighting, either against each other or against their own allies when they revolted. Throughout this time, they developed their military resources and experience, practising amid real dangers.

Chapter 19

§1 The Lacedaemonians led their allies without requiring tribute. They only took care that their allies were governed by oligarchies favourable to Sparta. The Athenians, by contrast, gradually took over the cities’ ships, except those of Chios and Lesbos, and required them all to pay money. By the start of this war, their own military resources were greater than at their height when the alliance was still intact.

Chapter 20

§1 These are my findings about the ancient past, though it is difficult to rely on every piece of evidence. People accept accounts of earlier events from one another without checking them, even when they concern their own country.

§2 Most Athenians, for instance, believe that Hipparchus was tyrant when Harmodius and Aristogiton killed him. They do not know that Hippias, the eldest son of Pisistratus, was the ruler, and that Hipparchus and Thessalus were his brothers. On the day of the attack, Harmodius and Aristogiton suddenly suspected that one of their fellow conspirators had informed Hippias. Believing him forewarned, they left him alone. But they wanted to accomplish something before being arrested, even at their own risk. They came across Hipparchus near the place called the Leocorium, arranging the Panathenaic procession, and killed him.

§3 Other Greeks also hold mistaken beliefs about many things that still exist and have not been forgotten with time. They think, for example, that each Lacedaemonian king has two votes rather than one, and that the Lacedaemonians have a Pitanate company, which has never existed. Most people take so little trouble to find the truth; they prefer to accept whatever account is readily available.

Chapter 21

§1 Even so, on the evidence I have given, someone who accepts that events broadly followed the course I have described will not be far wrong. There is less reason to trust the poets, who magnified these events with their embellishments, or the prose chroniclers, who aimed to please their listeners rather than tell the truth. Their accounts cannot be checked, and time has carried most of them beyond credibility into legend. My account should be considered sufficiently established from the clearest evidence available for events so long ago.

§2 People always judge the war they are fighting to be the greatest, then admire the wars of the distant past more once it is over. Nevertheless, those who examine the actual events will find that this war was greater than its predecessors.

Chapter 22

§1 As for the speeches delivered before the war or during it, it was difficult to remember the exact words, both for me when I heard them myself and for those who reported them to me from elsewhere. I have presented each speaker as saying what I thought the situation most called for, while keeping as close as possible to the overall meaning of what was actually said.

§2 As for the events of the war, I did not think it right to record whatever I heard from a chance informant or whatever seemed true to me. I investigated each event as accurately as I could, whether I had witnessed it myself or learned of it from others.

§3 Establishing the facts took considerable effort. Eyewitnesses gave different accounts of the same events, depending on their sympathies for one side or the other, or on their memories.

§4 The absence of legendary material may make this account less enjoyable to hear. But it will be enough if those who want a clear understanding of past events, and of similar events that human nature will bring about again, judge it useful. It has been composed as a possession for all time rather than a performance to win a moment’s hearing.

Chapter 23

§1 The greatest of the earlier events was the Median War, yet even that was quickly decided in two battles at sea and two on land. This war lasted a long time, and Greece suffered calamities unlike any others in a period of equal length.

§2 Never had so many cities been captured and left desolate, some by barbarians and others by the Greeks themselves fighting one another. Some cities even changed their inhabitants after being captured. Nor had so many people been driven into exile or killed, some in the war itself and others in civil wars.

§3 Stories that had once been passed down by word of mouth but rarely confirmed by events no longer seemed incredible. Earthquakes struck the widest areas at once and with the greatest violence. Eclipses of the sun occurred more often than was remembered from earlier times. Some places suffered severe droughts and the famines they caused. There was also the epidemic, which did immense harm and destroyed part of the population. All these disasters struck together with this war.

§4 The Athenians and the Peloponnesians began the war by breaking the thirty years’ truce they had made after the capture of Euboea.

§5 To explain why they broke it, I have first set out their grievances and disputes, so that no one need ever ask how so great a war came upon the Greeks.

§6 I believe the truest cause, though the least openly stated, was the growth of Athenian power and the fear it caused the Lacedaemonians, which forced them into war. The grievances openly stated by each side, which led them to break the truce and go to war, were as follows.

Chapter 24

§1 Epidamnus is a city on the right as you sail into the Ionian Gulf. Its neighbours are the Taulantii, a barbarian people of Illyria.

§2 The Corcyraeans established the colony. Its founder was Phalius, son of Eratocleides, a Corinthian descended from Heracles, invited from the mother city according to ancient custom. Some Corinthians and other Dorians also joined the settlement.

§3 Over time, Epidamnus grew powerful and populous.

§4 After many years of internal strife, it is said, a war with the neighbouring barbarians brought the people to ruin and deprived them of their former great power.

§5 Shortly before this war, the common people expelled the powerful citizens. The exiles joined the barbarians and raided those still in the city, by land and sea.

§6 Under severe pressure, the Epidamnians in the city sent envoys to their mother city, Corcyra. They begged the Corcyraeans not to stand by while they were destroyed, but to reconcile them with the exiles and end the war with the barbarians.

§7 They made these requests as suppliants seated in the sanctuary of Hera. The Corcyraeans refused their appeal and sent them away empty-handed.

Chapter 25

§1 When the Epidamnians realized that no help would come from Corcyra, they did not know how to deal with their predicament. They sent to Delphi to ask the god whether they should hand the city over to the Corinthians as its founders and seek help from them. The god told them to hand it over and take the Corinthians as their leaders.

§2 The Epidamnians therefore went to Corinth and handed over the colony in accordance with the oracle. They pointed out that their founder came from Corinth, reported the oracle’s answer and begged the Corinthians to help them instead of standing by while they were destroyed.

§3 The Corinthians agreed to give aid as a matter of justice, considering the colony as much theirs as Corcyra’s. They were also moved by hatred of the Corcyraeans, who disregarded Corinth despite being its colonists.

§4 The Corcyraeans gave them neither the customary honours at shared festivals nor the right for a Corinthian to begin the sacrifices, as the other colonies did. They despised the Corinthians. At that time their wealth equalled that of the richest Greeks, and they were stronger in military resources. At times they even boasted of their far greater naval strength, also invoking the fame of the Phaeacians, Corcyra’s earlier inhabitants, as seafarers. This encouraged them to develop their fleet further, and it was formidable: they had a hundred and twenty triremes when they began the war.

Chapter 26

§1 With all these grievances against Corcyra, the Corinthians readily sent help to Epidamnus. They invited anyone who wished to go as a settler and sent a garrison of Ambraciots, Leucadians and their own citizens.

§2 They travelled overland to Apollonia, a Corinthian colony, fearing that the Corcyraeans would intercept them if they crossed by sea.

§3 The Corcyraeans were furious when they learned that the settlers and garrison had reached Epidamnus and that the colony had been handed to Corinth. They immediately sailed there with twenty-five ships, followed later by another squadron. They arrogantly ordered the Epidamnians to take back their exiles and dismiss the garrison and settlers sent by Corinth. The exiles had come to Corcyra, pointing to ancestral graves and family ties and appealing on those grounds to be restored to their city.

§4 The Epidamnians rejected all their demands. The Corcyraeans then attacked them with forty ships, bringing the exiles to restore them and enlisting the Illyrians as well.

§5 Taking up positions outside the city, they announced that any Epidamnian who wished, as well as the foreigners, could leave unharmed. Anyone who stayed would be treated as an enemy. When the inhabitants refused, the Corcyraeans besieged the city, which stands on an isthmus.

Chapter 27

§1 When messengers from Epidamnus told the Corinthians that the city was under siege, they prepared an expedition. At the same time, they announced that anyone could join the colony at Epidamnus on equal terms. Those who wanted a share in the colony but did not wish to sail immediately could stay behind by depositing fifty Corinthian drachmas. Many volunteered to sail, and many others paid the money.

§2 They asked the Megarians to provide a naval escort in case the Corcyraeans tried to stop the voyage. The Megarians prepared eight ships, and the people of Pala in Cephallenia four. The Corinthians also asked Epidaurus, which supplied five ships; Hermione supplied one, Troezen two, Leucas ten and Ambracia eight. They asked Thebes and Phlius for money, and Elis for ships without crews as well as money. The Corinthians themselves prepared thirty ships and three thousand hoplites.

Chapter 28

§1 When the Corcyraeans learned of these preparations, they went to Corinth accompanied by Lacedaemonian and Sicyonian envoys whom they had persuaded to join them. They demanded that Corinth withdraw its garrison and settlers from Epidamnus, claiming that it had no right to the city.

§2 If the Corinthians disputed this, the Corcyraeans were willing to submit the matter to arbitration before Peloponnesian cities agreed on by both sides. Whichever side was awarded the colony would control it. They were also willing to entrust the decision to the oracle at Delphi.

§3 They urged the Corinthians not to start a war. Otherwise, they said, Corinth’s use of force would compel them to seek help from new friends, different from those they had now, whom they would not otherwise choose.

§4 The Corinthians replied that they would consider the proposal if the Corcyraeans withdrew their ships and the barbarians from Epidamnus. Until then, it would be wrong for them to submit to arbitration while the people in the city remained under siege.

§5 The Corcyraeans answered that they would withdraw if the Corinthians also removed their people from Epidamnus. Alternatively, they were ready for both sides to remain in position under a truce until the case had been decided.

Chapter 29

§1 The Corinthians accepted none of these proposals. Once their ships were fully manned and their allies had arrived, they sent a herald ahead to declare war on Corcyra. Then they set sail for Epidamnus with seventy-five ships and two thousand hoplites to fight the Corcyraeans.

§2 The fleet was commanded by Aristeus, son of Pellichus, Callicrates, son of Callias, and Timanor, son of Timanthes. The land force was commanded by Archetimus, son of Eurytimus, and Isarchidas, son of Isarchus.

§3 They reached Actium in the territory of Anactorium, where the sanctuary of Apollo stands at the entrance to the Ambracian Gulf. The Corcyraeans sent a herald to them in a small boat, warning them not to sail against Corcyra. Meanwhile they manned their own ships, having reinforced the older vessels to make them seaworthy and repaired the others.

§4 The herald returned with no peaceful answer from Corinth. The Corcyraeans had now manned eighty ships; another forty were besieging Epidamnus. They sailed out to meet the Corinthians, formed their battle line and fought at sea.

§5 The Corcyraeans won decisively and destroyed fifteen Corinthian ships. That same day, their forces besieging Epidamnus also secured the city’s surrender. The terms were that the newcomers would be sold into slavery, while the Corinthians would be kept in chains until a further decision was made.

Chapter 30

§1 After the battle, the Corcyraeans set up a victory trophy at Leukimme, a headland on Corcyra. They killed all the prisoners they had taken except the Corinthians, whom they kept in chains.

§2 Later, when the defeated Corinthians and their allies sailed home, the Corcyraeans controlled all the sea in that region. They sailed to Leucas, Corinth’s colony, and ravaged its territory. They also burned Cyllene, the port of Elis, because the Eleians had supplied Corinth with ships and money.

§3 For most of the period after the battle, they controlled the sea and raided Corinth’s allies. Towards the end of summer, the Corinthians sent ships and troops because their allies were suffering. They camped at Actium and near Cheimerium in Thesprotis to protect Leucas and the other friendly cities.

§4 The Corcyraeans took up a position opposite them at Leukimme with ships and land forces. Neither side sailed out to attack the other. They remained facing one another that summer, and both returned home when winter had begun.

Chapter 31

§1 Throughout the year after the naval battle and the following year, the Corinthians, bitter over the war with Corcyra, built ships and prepared the strongest fleet they could. They recruited rowers from the Peloponnese itself and the rest of Greece by offering them pay.

§2 The Corcyraeans were alarmed when they learned of these preparations. They had no treaty with any Greek state and had joined neither the Athenian nor the Lacedaemonian alliance. They therefore decided to approach the Athenians, become their allies and try to obtain help from them.

§3 When the Corinthians heard this, they too sent an embassy to Athens. They wanted to prevent the Athenian fleet from joining the Corcyraean fleet and keeping them from settling the war on their own terms.

§4 An assembly was called, and the two sides presented their opposing cases. The Corcyraeans spoke as follows:

Chapter 32

§1 Athenians, when people come to others asking for help, as we do now, without having earned it by some great service or a prior alliance, they should first explain their request. Above all, they must show that it also benefits those they approach, or at least does them no harm. Next, they must show that their gratitude will last. If they cannot make any of this clear, they have no right to be angry when their request fails.

§2 The Corcyraeans have sent us to request an alliance, confident that we can also give you firm assurances on these points.

§3 Our policy has made our present appeal to you seem unreasonable and has also harmed our own interests in the current situation.

§4 We deliberately stayed out of every alliance in the past, yet now we have come to ask others for one. That same policy has left us alone in the present war with Corinth. We once thought it prudent to avoid alliances with other states, so that we would not have to share risks dictated by someone else. That policy now looks like folly and weakness.

§5 In the naval battle already fought, we repelled the Corinthians by ourselves. But they are now advancing against us with a larger force from the Peloponnese and the rest of Greece. We see that our own strength alone cannot defeat them, and falling under their power would put us in great danger. We have no choice but to ask for help from you and anyone else. You should forgive us for daring to abandon our former policy of keeping out of others’ affairs: this change comes from an error of judgement, not bad intent.

Chapter 33

§1 If you agree, our need will offer you a fine opportunity in several ways. First, you will help people who are being wronged and are doing no harm to others. Next, by accepting us when everything we value is at stake, you will secure our lasting gratitude through an act that will never be forgotten. We also possess the largest fleet apart from yours.

§2 Consider how rare such good fortune is, and how unwelcome to your enemies. A force whose support you would have valued at the cost of much money and many favours now offers itself to you uninvited, without risk or expense. It also brings you a reputation for virtue in the eyes of the public, gratitude from those you help and greater strength for yourselves. Few people in all history have enjoyed all these advantages at once. Few who seek an alliance come offering those they approach as much security and honour as they hope to receive.

§3 If any of you thinks that the war in which we could prove useful will not happen, he is mistaken. He fails to see that fear of you is driving the Lacedaemonians towards war, and that the Corinthians, who have influence with them and are your enemies, are trying to seize us now in preparation for an attack on you. They want to prevent us from joining you against them in our common hostility, and to make sure they do not miss the chance either to weaken us or to strengthen their own position.

§4 Our task now is to act first: we should offer the alliance and you should accept it. We should forestall their plans against us, rather than merely respond to them.

Chapter 34

§1 If they say you have no right to accept their colonists, let them learn that every colony honours its mother city when treated well, but becomes estranged when wronged. Colonists are sent out to be equals of those who stay behind, not their slaves.

§2 Their wrongdoing is plain. When we challenged them to submit the dispute over Epidamnus to arbitration, they chose to pursue their grievances through war rather than a fair settlement.

§3 Take their treatment of us, their own kinsmen, as a warning. Do not let them mislead you by deceit or persuade you to comply with their outright requests. Those who give their opponents the fewest favours they will later regret are the most likely to remain secure.

Chapter 35

§1 Accepting us will not break your treaty with the Lacedaemonians, since we are allied with neither side.

§2 The treaty expressly allows any Greek city that belongs to neither alliance to join whichever side it chooses.

§3 It would be outrageous to let these men recruit crews from their treaty partners, the rest of Greece and, not least, your own subjects, while they shut us out of the alliance we propose and any other source of help. Yet they would then accuse you of wrongdoing if you granted our request.

§4 We would have far more reason to blame you if we failed to persuade you. You would reject us, who are in danger and are not your enemies. Meanwhile, you would allow these men, who are your enemies and are advancing against us, not only to proceed unchecked but even to draw reinforcements from your empire. That is unjust. You should either stop them recruiting mercenaries from your territory or send us whatever help you agree to give. Best of all, you should openly accept us and come to our aid.

§5 As we said at the outset, we can show you many advantages. The greatest is that we have the same enemies, the surest guarantee of loyalty. These enemies are strong enough to harm anyone who changes sides. Moreover, the alliance we offer is with a naval power, not a land power, so rejecting it has different consequences. Your best course, if possible, is to let no one else possess a fleet. Failing that, make a friend of the strongest naval power.

Chapter 36

§1 Some of you may see the advantages in our argument but fear that accepting it will break the treaty. Such a man should understand that caution backed by strength will frighten his opponents more, while confidence without that strength, if he rejects us, will leave powerful enemies less afraid. He should also recognize that he is deciding the fate of Athens no less than that of Corcyra. He is not serving Athens well if concern for the immediate moment makes him hesitate to secure this position for the war that is coming and is almost upon us. Having it as a friend or an enemy makes an enormous difference.

§2 For our island is well placed on the sea route to Italy and Sicily. It can prevent a fleet from coming from there to help the Peloponnesians, and escort a fleet from here to those regions. It is also exceptionally useful for other purposes.

§3 A very brief argument should show you, both as a community and as individuals, why you should not abandon us. There are three Greek fleets worth mentioning: yours, ours and the Corinthians'. If you allow two of them to come under the same control and the Corinthians seize us first, you will have to fight both Corcyraeans and Peloponnesians at sea. If you accept us, however, you will be able to fight them with a larger fleet, thanks to our ships.

§4 That was the Corcyraeans’ case. The Corinthians then spoke as follows:

Chapter 37

§1 These Corcyraeans have spoken not only to ask you to accept them, but also to claim that we are wronging them and waging an unjust war against them. We must first address both points before moving on to the rest of our case. You will then understand our claim more securely and have sound reasons for rejecting their request.

§2 They say that prudence has kept them out of alliances until now. In fact, they followed this policy to do wrong, not to act virtuously. They wanted neither allies nor witnesses to their misdeeds, nor the embarrassment of calling others in.

§3 Their city’s self-sufficient location also lets them judge for themselves the wrongs they do to others, instead of submitting to agreed procedures. They very seldom sail to other places, while other people are very often forced to put in at their port.

§4 Their supposedly respectable refusal to make treaties is a cover. Its purpose is not to avoid sharing in others’ wrongdoing, but to do wrong on their own: to use force where they have the upper hand, take advantage where they can escape notice and feel no shame about anything they acquire.

§5 Yet if they were good men, as they claim, the harder it was for their neighbours to bring them to account, the more clearly they could have shown their virtue by giving and receiving fair treatment.

Chapter 38

§1 But that is not how they treat others or us. Although they are our colonists, they have always kept their distance and are now fighting us. They claim they were not sent out to suffer mistreatment.

§2 We likewise maintain that we did not found their colony to be insulted by them, but to be their leaders and receive the respect we deserve.

§3 Our other colonies honour us, and our colonists are particularly fond of us.

§4 If we satisfy most of them, it is clearly unreasonable for these people alone to be dissatisfied. Nor would we make such an exceptional expedition against them unless we had suffered exceptional wrongs.

§5 Even if we had been at fault, it would have been honourable for them to give way to our anger. We, in turn, would have been ashamed to use force against their restraint.

§6 Instead, arrogance and the freedom their wealth gives them have led them to wrong us in many ways. Epidamnus belongs to us, yet they disowned it while it was suffering. Once we came to its aid, they seized it by force and now hold it.

Chapter 39

§1 They also say they had previously offered arbitration. But such an offer deserves to be taken seriously only from someone who puts actions as well as words on an equal footing before resorting to force, not from someone who already holds the advantage and issues the challenge from safety.

§2 They did not offer this respectable appearance of arbitration before besieging the city, but only after realizing that we would not stand by. Now, after their wrongdoing there, they have come here asking you to share in it, not simply to become their allies. They want you to accept them while they are in conflict with us.

§3 They should have approached you when their position was secure, not now, when we have been wronged and they are in danger. You had no share in their strength then, yet now you would give them help. You took no part in their wrongdoing, yet we would blame you equally for it. They should have shared their strength with you long ago if they wanted to share the consequences too.

Chapter 40

§1 We have shown that our grievances are justified and that these men act with violence and greed. You must now understand why it would be unjust to accept them.

§2 The treaty does allow cities outside the alliances to join whichever side they choose. But that provision is for those who seek security without breaking away from another power, not those who join to harm others. Nor, if the receiving side acts sensibly, should they bring it war in place of peace. That is what could happen to you now if you disregard our advice.

§3 You would become not only their helpers but our enemies, instead of our treaty partners. If you go with them, we will have to fight you as well as them.

§4 The fairest course is to keep out of both sides’ way. Otherwise, you should take the opposite course and join us against them. You have a treaty with Corinth, but have never had even an armistice with Corcyra. Do not establish a rule of accepting those who revolt from others.

§5 When the Samians revolted against you, we did not cast our vote against you. The other Peloponnesians were divided over whether to help them, but we openly opposed intervention, arguing that each power should punish its own allies.

§6 If you accept and help wrongdoers, you will find that just as many of your own people will come over to us. The rule you establish will turn against you more than against us.

Chapter 41

§1 These claims are sufficient under Greek law. But we also offer advice and ask you to repay a favour. We are neither enemies seeking to harm you nor friends claiming too much from your friendship. We believe you should repay us now on the following grounds.

§2 Before the Median War, when you lacked warships in your war against Aegina, you received twenty ships from Corinth. That service enabled you to defeat the Aeginetans. Another service, our preventing the Peloponnesians from helping Samos, enabled you to punish the Samians. Both came at moments when people fighting their enemies care about nothing but victory.

§3 At such times they consider anyone who helps them a friend, even a former enemy, and anyone who opposes them an enemy, even a friend. The desire to win at once makes them neglect even their own interests.

Chapter 42

§1 Remember these things, and let younger men learn them from their elders. You should consider it right to repay us in kind. Do not think that our argument is just but that, if war comes, your interests will lie elsewhere.

§2 Your interests are best served by making as few mistakes as possible. The future war with which the Corcyraeans frighten you into wrongdoing is still uncertain. It is not worth being carried away by that prospect and making Corinth your open enemy now, instead of at some future time. It would be wiser to reduce the suspicion that already exists because of the Megarians. For the latest favour, if well timed,

§3 can settle a greater grievance even if it is itself small.

§4 Nor should their offer of a powerful naval alliance tempt you. Treating your equals fairly gives you a firmer basis of strength than being carried away by an immediate, visible advantage and taking risks to gain more.

Chapter 43

§1 We now face the very situation we spoke about at Lacedaemon, when we said that each power should punish its own allies. We ask you to give us the same treatment. Our vote benefited you; do not use yours to harm us.

§2 Repay us in kind. Recognize that this is precisely the moment when the person who helps is most truly a friend, and the person who opposes is an enemy.

§3 Do not accept these Corcyraeans as allies against our wishes or help them in their wrongdoing.

§4 By following this course, you will act rightly and make the best decision for yourselves.

Chapter 44

§1 That was the Corinthians’ case. The Athenians heard both sides in two assemblies. At the first, they approved the Corinthian arguments at least as much as the Corcyraean ones. At the second, they changed their minds. They decided against an alliance that would commit them to sharing Corcyra’s friends and enemies: if the Corcyraeans asked them to sail against Corinth, that would break their treaty with the Peloponnesians. Instead, they made a defensive alliance, agreeing to help one another if anyone attacked Corcyra, Athens or the allies of either.

§2 They believed that war with the Peloponnesians would come in any case, and they did not want to abandon Corcyra and its large fleet to Corinth. They wanted the two sides to clash as much as possible, so that, if necessary, they could go to war against Corinth and the other naval powers after these had been weakened.

§3 They also considered the island well placed on the sea route to Italy and Sicily.

Chapter 45

§1 These were the reasons why the Athenians accepted the Corcyraeans. Shortly after the Corinthians left, they sent ten ships to help Corcyra.

§2 The commanders were Lacedaemonius, son of Cimon, Diotimus, son of Strombichus, and Proteas, son of Epicles.

§3 Their orders were not to fight the Corinthians at sea unless they sailed against Corcyra and were about to land there or in some other Corcyraean territory. In that case, they were to stop them with all the strength they could. These instructions were intended to avoid breaking the treaty.

Chapter 46

§1 The ships reached Corcyra. Once their preparations were complete, the Corinthians sailed against Corcyra with a hundred and fifty ships: ten from Elis, twelve from Megara, ten from Leucas, twenty-seven from Ambracia, one from Anactorium and ninety of their own.

§2 Each city's ships had their own commanders. The Corinthians were commanded by Xenocleides son of Euthycles and four colleagues.

§3 Sailing from Leucas, they reached the mainland opposite Corcyra and anchored at Cheimerium in Thesprotis.

§4 There is a harbour there. Above it, inland from the sea, stands Ephyre, a city in the Eleatis district of Thesprotis. Beside the city, the Acherusian lake drains into the sea. The river Acheron flows through Thesprotis into the lake, which takes its name from the river. The river Thyamis also flows there, marking the boundary between Thesprotis and Cestrine. Between the two rivers, the headland of Cheimerium projects into the sea.

§5 The Corinthians anchored at this point on the mainland and set up camp.

Chapter 47

§1 When the Corcyraeans learned that the enemy was approaching by sea, they manned a hundred and ten ships under Miciades, Aesimides and Eurybatus. They camped on one of the islands called Sybota. The ten Attic ships were there too.

§2 Their land force was at Cape Leukimme, together with a thousand Zacynthian hoplites who had come to help them.

§3 Many barbarians had also come to help the Corinthians on the mainland. The mainland peoples of that region have always been friendly to them.

Chapter 48

§1 Once ready, the Corinthians took aboard three days’ provisions and sailed from Cheimerium at night to fight a naval battle.

§2 At daybreak, while still sailing, they saw the Corcyraean ships out at sea and coming towards them.

§3 When the fleets sighted each other, they formed opposing battle lines. The Attic ships held the Corcyraean right wing. The Corcyraeans themselves held the rest of the line, dividing their ships into three squadrons, each commanded by one of the three generals.

§4 That was the Corcyraean formation. On the Corinthian side, the Megarian and Ambraciot ships held the right wing. The other allies were in the centre, each in their own contingent. The Corinthians themselves held the left with their best-sailing ships, opposite the Athenians and the Corcyraean right wing.

Chapter 49

§1 Once the signals were raised on both sides, the fleets closed and fought. Both carried many hoplites, archers and javelin-throwers on their decks, still equipped for the old, less skilful form of naval warfare.

§2 The battle was fierce but showed less skill than determination. It largely resembled a battle on land.

§3 Once the ships collided, the sheer number and crowding of the vessels made it hard to pull apart. Both sides relied mainly on their deck hoplites for victory. These men fought from their positions while the ships remained still. There were no manoeuvres to break through the enemy line; the battle depended more on spirit and strength than on expertise.

§4 The battle was noisy and confused everywhere. The Attic ships moved up wherever the Corcyraeans were hard pressed, frightening the enemy, but did not initiate fighting. Their commanders were afraid of disobeying the Athenians’ instructions.

§5 The Corinthian right wing suffered most. Twenty Corcyraean ships routed it, pursued the scattered enemy vessels to the mainland and sailed right up to their camp. The Corcyraeans landed, burned the empty tents and plundered the property.

§6 On that wing, the Corinthians and their allies were losing and the Corcyraeans prevailed. But on the left, where the Corinthians themselves were stationed, they won decisively. The Corcyraean fleet, already smaller, was further reduced by the absence of the twenty ships engaged in pursuit.

§7 Seeing the Corcyraeans under pressure, the Athenians now helped them with fewer reservations. At first they still held back from ramming any ship. But once the rout was clear and the Corinthians pressed on, everyone joined the fighting and distinctions disappeared. Events forced matters to the point where Corinthians and Athenians attacked one another.

Chapter 50

§1 After the rout, the Corinthians did not fasten ropes to the hulls of the ships they had sunk and take them in tow. Instead, they sailed through the wreckage, concentrating on killing the men rather than taking them prisoner. They even killed their own friends without recognizing them, unaware that their right wing had been defeated.

§2 Both sides had many ships, spread over a wide area of sea. Once they met, it was hard to distinguish who was winning and who was losing. In the number of ships involved, this was the greatest naval battle yet fought between Greeks.

§3 After pursuing the Corcyraeans to the shore, the Corinthians turned to the wrecks and their own dead. They recovered most of them and brought them to Sybota, where the barbarian land force had arrived to help them. Sybota is an uninhabited harbour in Thesprotis. Once this was done, they regrouped and sailed against the Corcyraeans again.

§4 The Corcyraeans sailed out to meet them with their seaworthy ships and all the others they had left, together with the Attic ships. They feared an attempt to land on their territory.

§5 It was already late, and the battle song had been sung for the attack. Suddenly the Corinthians began to row backwards: they had spotted twenty Athenian ships approaching. The Athenians had sent these after the first ten as reinforcements. They feared, as proved to be the case, that the Corcyraeans would be defeated and their own ten ships would be too few to defend them.

Chapter 51

§1 The Corinthians, then, catching sight of these ships from afar, and suspecting that those from Athens were not merely as many as they saw but more, began to fall back.

§2 But the ships, as they bore down upon the Corcyraeans, were not seen by them, for they were sailing rather out of a quarter where they could not be made out; and the Corcyraeans were astonished at the Corinthians backing water, until some, perceiving them, said that yonder ships were sailing in. Then they too drew off; for it was now growing dark, and the Corinthians, turning about, brought the engagement to an end.

§3 In this way the two sides parted from one another, and the sea-fight came to an end with nightfall.

§4 While the Corcyraeans lay encamped at Leukimme, these twenty ships from Athens, which were commanded by Glaucon son of Leagrus and Andocides son of Leogoras, made their way through the corpses and the wrecks, and put in to the camp not long after they had first been sighted.

§5 The Corcyraeans — it was night — feared that the ships might be enemies, but presently recognized them; and they came to anchor.

Chapter 52

§1 On the next day the thirty Athenian ships put out from the land, together with as many of the Corcyraean ships as were still seaworthy, and sailed against the harbour at Sybota, in which the Corinthians lay at anchor, wishing to find out whether they would give battle.

§2 The Corinthians for their part put out from the land, and, having drawn up their ships in line in the offing, kept quiet, not intending of their own accord to begin a sea-fight — seeing both that fresh ships besides had arrived from Athens, and that many difficulties had befallen themselves: the prisoners whom they held in their ships, who required guarding, and the lack of any means of repairing the ships in a desolate place.

§3 But rather they were giving their minds to the voyage home, and to how they should get away; for they feared that, if the Athenians judged the truce to have been broken because the two sides had come to blows, they would not let them put out to sea.

Chapter 53

§1 It was decided, accordingly, to put men aboard a skiff, and send them to the Athenians without a herald’s staff, and make trial of them; and the men sent spoke as follows.

§2 “You are doing wrong, men of Athens, in beginning a war and breaking the truce; for as we punish our enemies you stand in our path, taking up arms against us in return. If, then, it is your resolve to prevent us from sailing against Corcyra or anywhere else we may wish to sail, and you are breaking the truce, then take us, the men here before you, first, and deal with us as enemies.”

§3 So much they said; and the Corcyraean army, as many of them as were within hearing, at once shouted out that the men should be seized and put to death; and the Athenians made this answer.

§4 “We are neither beginning a war, men of the Peloponnese, nor are we breaking the truce, but we have come to the help of these our allies the Corcyraeans. If, then, you wish to sail anywhere else, we do not hinder you; but if you are for sailing against Corcyra, or against any territory of theirs, we shall not stand by and look on, so far as in us lies.”

Chapter 54

§1 Such was the answer the Athenians gave; and the Corinthians made ready for the voyage home and set up a trophy at Sybota on the mainland, while the Corcyraeans took up the wrecks and corpses that had been carried over to their side by the current and by the wind — which had risen in the night and scattered them in every direction — and set up a trophy in answer at Sybota by the island, as being themselves the victors.

§2 On grounds such as these each side made the victory its own. The Corinthians had been masters of the sea-fight down to nightfall — so that they also brought in very many wrecks and corpses — and were holding no fewer than a thousand men as prisoners, and had sunk about seventy ships; and they set up a trophy. The Corcyraeans had destroyed about thirty ships; and, once the Athenians had come, they picked up the wrecks and corpses on their own side; and the Corinthians on the previous day had backed water and given way before them when they saw the Attic ships, and when the Athenians came did not sail out against them from Sybota; and for these reasons they set up a trophy.

Chapter 55

§1 On these grounds, then, each side claimed the victory; and the Corinthians, on their voyage home, took Anactorium — it lies at the mouth of the Ambracian gulf, and was a joint possession of the Corcyraeans and themselves — by a ruse, and, having established Corinthians in it as settlers, withdrew homewards. Of the Corcyraeans they had taken, they sold eight hundred who were slaves, but two hundred and fifty they kept bound and under guard, treating them with much attention, that on their return home they might win Corcyra over to the Corinthians; and most of them, as it happened, were the first men of the city in power as well.

§2 So, then, Corcyra got the better of the Corinthians in the war, and the ships of the Athenians withdrew from the island; and this was the first cause of war for the Corinthians against the Athenians: that, while the truce was in force, the Athenians had fought at sea against them together with the Corcyraeans.

Chapter 56

§1 Immediately after these events, the following also occurred, which became matters of difference between the Athenians and the Peloponnesians, driving them towards war.

§2 For the Corinthians were busy contriving how they might punish them; and the Athenians, suspecting their enmity, ordered the Potidaeans — who dwell on the isthmus of Pallene, colonists of the Corinthians but allies of their own, and tributary — to raze the wall that faces Pallene, to give hostages, to send away the magistrates sent to them for the year, and henceforth to receive no more of those whom the Corinthians sent them year by year. For they feared that, persuaded both by Perdiccas and by the Corinthians, the Potidaeans might revolt and draw the rest of their allies in Thrace into revolt together.

Chapter 57

§1 These preparations against the Potidaeans the Athenians were making in advance, directly after the sea-battle at Corcyra;

§2 for the Corinthians were now already openly at variance with them, and Perdiccas son of Alexander, king of the Macedonians, had been made an enemy, although he had before been ally and friend.

§3 He had been made an enemy because the Athenians had contracted an alliance with Philip, his own brother, and with Derdas, who were jointly in opposition to him.

§4 In fear he was hard at work, sending to Lacedaemon so that a war might arise for the Athenians against the Peloponnesians, and working to attach the Corinthians to himself in the interest of the revolt of Potidaea;

§5 he made overtures besides to the Chalcidians in Thrace and to the Bottiaeans, that they should join in the revolt, judging that, if he had these men as allies, with their lands bordering on his own, he would more easily carry on the war in their company.

§6 The Athenians, perceiving all this and wishing to forestall the revolts of the cities — they happened at the time to be sending out thirty ships and one thousand hoplites against his territory, with Archestratus son of Lycomedes as general together with ten others — sent instructions to those in command of the ships, that they should take hostages from the Potidaeans and raze the wall, and that they should keep watch over the cities near by so that they should not revolt. [Note: Jones marks “ten” as textually doubtful. The number printed in the source is retained here.]

Chapter 58

§1 The Potidaeans, then, sent envoys to Athens as well, in the hope that they might persuade the Athenians to attempt nothing new against them; and envoys also went to Lacedaemon in company with the Corinthians, and laboured to have redress in readiness, should the need arise; for after long negotiation they had found nothing serviceable from Athens, and the ships that were bound against Macedonia were no less plainly on their way against themselves; and the authorities of the Lacedaemonians promised them that, if the Athenians should march against Potidaea, they would invade Attica. It was then, at that very juncture, that they revolted, having sworn together with the Chalcidians and Bottiaeans to a common oath.

§2 And Perdiccas induced the Chalcidians to abandon their towns on the sea-coast and, pulling them down, to re-settle themselves in Olynthus, and so to make of it one city, and a strong one; to the men who thus abandoned their homes he gave land of his own, Mygdonia about Lake Bolbe, to hold, for as long as the war against the Athenians should last. These men, then, were re-settling themselves, tearing down their towns, and making ready for war.

Chapter 59

§1 The thirty Athenian ships arrived in the parts of Thrace, and found Potidaea and the other places already in revolt.

§2 The generals, judging that with the force they had it was impossible to wage war upon Perdiccas and at the same time to master the places that had revolted in concert with him, turned against Macedonia, the very object for which they had originally been put into the field; and taking up a position there they carried on the war in company with Philip and the brothers of Derdas, who had already made an incursion with an army from the upper country.

Chapter 60

§1 Meanwhile the Corinthians, with Potidaea already in revolt and the Athenian ships engaged about Macedonia, were fearful for the place and counted the danger as their own; so they sent volunteers from their own citizens, and from the rest of the Peloponnesians men whom they had induced by pay — one thousand six hundred hoplites in all, and four hundred light-armed.

§2 Aristeus son of Adimantus had the command of them; and it was not least because of their friendship for him that the greater number of the volunteer soldiers from Corinth joined the expedition; for he had always been on intimate terms with the Potidaeans.

§3 And they arrived in the parts of Thrace on the fortieth day after the revolt of Potidaea.

Chapter 61

§1 News of the cities' revolt reached the Athenians at once. When they learned that Aristeus and his men were also approaching, they sent two thousand of their own hoplites and forty ships against the rebellious cities, under Callias son of Calliades and four fellow generals.

§2 Arriving in Macedonia, they first found the thousand men who had been sent out before them, who had just captured Therma and were besieging Pydna.

§3 They themselves sat down before Pydna too and besieged it; but presently, having made an agreement and an alliance of necessity with Perdiccas — for Potidaea pressed upon them and Aristeus had now arrived — they withdrew from Macedonia.

§4 Coming to Beroea and thence upon Strepsa, they first made trial of the place and, failing to take it, marched on by land against Potidaea, three thousand hoplites of their own besides a large number of allies, with six hundred Macedonian horsemen, those under Philip and Pausanias; and seventy ships sailed along with them.

§5 Advancing by short stages, on the third day they arrived at Gigonus and pitched camp.

Chapter 62

§1 The Potidaeans and the Peloponnesians under Aristeus, as they awaited the Athenians, had their camp on the isthmus towards Olynthus, and had established a market outside the city.

§2 Of all the infantry the allies had chosen Aristeus as their commander, and of the cavalry Perdiccas; for Perdiccas had immediately revolted once more from the Athenians and was fighting on the side of the Potidaeans, having installed Iolaus as ruler in his own place.

§3 The plan of Aristeus was to keep watch on the Athenians on the isthmus with the army he had there, in case they came on, while the Chalcidians and the allies outside the isthmus and the two hundred cavalry furnished by Perdiccas should remain at Olynthus, so that, whenever the Athenians marched against Aristeus, these might come to his aid from the rear and so place the enemy in the middle of their own two forces.

§4 Callias the Athenian general and his fellow commanders sent the Macedonian cavalry and a few of the allies to Olynthus, to stop the men there from bringing help, while they themselves broke up their camp and marched upon Potidaea.

§5 And when they came to the isthmus and saw the enemy forming up for battle, they too drew themselves up to face them, and not long afterwards the two forces closed.

§6 Aristeus' own wing, with the picked men of the Corinthians and of the rest who were gathered about him, routed the troops facing them and went far in pursuit; but the rest of the army, of Potidaeans and Peloponnesians, was worsted by the Athenians and fled inside the wall.

Chapter 63

§1 When Aristeus turned back from the pursuit and saw that the rest of the army had been worsted, he was at a loss which of the two risks he should run, whether to make for Olynthus or into Potidaea. It seemed best to him to gather the men with him into as narrow a space as possible and force a way at the run into Potidaea; and he got through along the mole, through the sea, under fire and with difficulty, losing a few of his men but saving most.

§2 The men from Olynthus who were to bring help to the Potidaeans — Olynthus is distant about sixty stades at most, and is in plain sight — as soon as the battle was joined and the signals were raised, advanced a little way, meaning to bring help, and the Macedonian cavalry formed up opposite to stop them; but when the Athenian victory was decided with speed and the signals were hauled down, they retired again within the wall, and the Macedonians went back to the Athenians; of cavalry there were none present on either side in the battle.

§3 After the battle the Athenians set up a trophy and gave back the dead to the Potidaeans under truce. There fell of the Potidaeans and their allies a little fewer than three hundred; of the Athenians themselves one hundred and fifty, and the general Callias.

Chapter 64

§1 On the side of the isthmus the Athenians at once built a wall across and garrisoned it; towards Pallene there was none as yet; for they did not think themselves sufficient both to hold the isthmus with a garrison and, after crossing into Pallene, to build there as well, fearing that the Potidaeans and their allies might attack them while their forces were divided in two.

§2 The Athenians at home, learning that the Pallene side was still without a wall, sent after an interval one thousand six hundred hoplites of their own and the general Phormio son of Asopius. He, arriving in Pallene and making Aphytis his base, brought his army up against Potidaea, advancing little by little and ravaging the country as he went; and when no one came out against him to give battle, he walled the Pallene side.

§3 And in this way Potidaea was now being besieged with full force from both sides, while ships lay at anchor off it by sea.

Chapter 65

§1 When the city had been walled in, Aristeus, having no hope of safety unless something should come from the Peloponnese or some other thing beyond expectation occur, advised all except five hundred to watch for a wind and sail out, so that the corn might last longer; and he was willing himself to be among those who stayed. Failing to persuade them, and wishing both to provide for what should follow on their account and to have matters outside in as good a state as possible, he sailed out, eluding the Athenians' guard.

§2 Staying on among the Chalcidians, he carried on the war in the other ways too, and, laying an ambush close to the city of the Sermylians, destroyed many of them; and towards the Peloponnese he was at work, wherever some advantage might arise.

§3 After the investment of Potidaea, Phormio, with his one thousand six hundred men, ravaged Chalcidice and Bottice, and he captured even some small towns.

Chapter 66

§1 Such, then, were the accusations which had arisen beforehand between the Athenians and the Peloponnesians on either side: on the part of the Corinthians, that the Athenians were laying siege to Potidaea, a colony of their own, and to Corinthians and Peloponnesians present within it; on the part of the Athenians against the Peloponnesians, that they had driven into revolt a city of the Athenians — one allied and paying tribute to them — and, coming out into the open, had fought against them at the side of the Potidaeans. Nevertheless the war had not yet broken out; an armistice still continued; for the Corinthians had done these things on their own initiative.

Chapter 67

§1 While Potidaea was still being besieged, the Corinthians were not idle — they had men of their own in the place and at the same time feared for it; they straightaway urged their allies to go to Lacedaemon, and, coming there, they cried out against the Athenians, declaring that the Athenians had broken the truce and were wronging the Peloponnese.

§2 The Aeginetans too, who sent no open embassy because they feared the Athenians, were in secret by no means the least active with the rest in goading on the war, saying that they were not autonomous according to the truce.

§3 The Lacedaemonians, having further called upon their allies and upon any others who said they had been wronged in some other way by the Athenians, convened a congress of their own, and bade them speak as was customary.

§4 Others also came forward, one after another, each pressing his own complaint; among them the Megarians, who set forth not a few other points of difference besides, and above all that they were shut out from the harbours in the Athenian empire and from the market of Attica contrary to the truce.

§5 The Corinthians came forward last of all — having let the others first provoke the Lacedaemonians — and spoke as follows.

Chapter 68

§1 Lacedaemonians, the trustworthiness of your own constitution and of the intercourse you keep among yourselves makes you slow to believe others whenever we say anything to you; and from it you have moderation, but to your conduct of affairs outside you bring a greater ignorance.

§2 For often, when we proclaimed beforehand the wrongs we were going to suffer at the hands of the Athenians, you did not apply your learning to the matters about which you were each time being instructed; you suspected rather that the speakers spoke for the sake of private differences of their own. And so you did not call these allies together before the suffering came, but only now that we stand in the midst of the thing itself — in a matter in which it belongs to us to speak not least, since we have the greatest complaints, outraged as we are by the Athenians and neglected by you.

§3 And if it were from some hidden quarter that they were wronging Hellas, instruction would be needed, as for men who did not know; but now, what need is there to speak at length? You see some already enslaved, and against others they are plotting — not least against our own allies — and they have long been prepared in advance, for whatever day it may be on which they go to war.

§4 For they would never have forestalled us in seizing Corcyra and held it by force, nor would they now be besieging Potidaea, were it not that the one is for them the most advantageous station for their undertakings towards Thrace, and the other would have furnished the Peloponnesians with the greatest fleet.

Chapter 69

§1 And for these things you are responsible — first, by allowing them to strengthen their city after the Median war and later to build the long walls; and down to this day you have always been robbing of freedom not only the states which they have enslaved, but even those who are by now your own allies; for not he who has done the enslaving, but he who could have put a stop to it and yet neglected it, more truly does the deed — if indeed the claim to virtue as the liberators of Hellas is to be upheld.

§2 Only with difficulty have we now come together, and not even now upon open grounds. It is no longer the time to consider whether we are being wronged, but how we are to defend ourselves; for those who act, with their plans already made, come upon men who have not yet resolved and are intending nothing.

§3 We know too by what way the Athenians advance, and that they encroach little by little upon their neighbours; and believing that they escape notice because of your want of perception, they are the less venturesome; but once they learn that you know it and yet look on, they will come on with all their strength.

§4 For you alone of the Greeks, Lacedaemonians, keep quiet — repelling no one by your power, but defending yourselves by delay; you alone do not put down the growth of your enemies at its beginning, but only when it has become double.

§5 And yet you were said to be safe — you of all men, in whom word has prevailed over deed. We ourselves know that the Mede came from the ends of the earth against the Peloponnese sooner than aught worthy of you went forth to meet him; and now you look on while the Athenians are not, as he was, far away, but close at hand; and instead of marching against them you choose rather to defend yourselves against them as they come on, and, contending against men far stronger, to commit yourselves to the chances — knowing that the barbarian too was brought down for the most part through his own mischances, and that against the very Athenians we have already many a time had the upper hand through their blunders rather than through any punishment of yours; for it is precisely your hopes that have already destroyed some men somewhere besides, unprepared because they trusted them.

§6 And let none of you suppose that these things are said more out of enmity than in the way of blame; for blame is the mark of friends who have slipped, while accusation belongs to enemies who have done wrong.

Chapter 70

§1 And at the same time, if others besides ourselves may claim the like, we hold ourselves entitled to bring a word of reproach upon our neighbours — all the more since great differences subsist, concerning which you seem to us at least not to be aware, nor ever yet to have reckoned against what sort of Athenians you will have to contend, and how far they are your superiors, and against how utterly different men the contest will be.

§2 They are innovators, quick to devise and quick to accomplish in action whatever they have resolved upon; you are for keeping safe what you already have, for taking in nothing new, and for not putting even what is necessary into execution.

§3 Then, too, they are venturesome beyond their power and risk-takers beyond their judgement, and hopeful amid dangers; your temper is to come short in action of what your power allows, in judgement to trust not even what is sure, and never to expect that you will escape from dangers.

§4 Besides, they are men of no shrinking beside you men of delay, and men who go abroad beside you the most stay-at-home of men; for they think that absence abroad may gain them something, while you think that by marching out you would damage even what you hold ready to your hand.

§5 When they have the upper hand of their enemies, they follow up to the utmost; when they are beaten, they give way but little.

§6 Their bodies, moreover, they use for the city as things most nearly not their own; their minds they use, for doing anything on her behalf, as most entirely their own.

§7 Whatever they have planned, if they fail to carry it through, they count themselves robbed of what was their own; what they gain by setting out after it they hold but small in view of what is still before them; and if they fail in some one trial, they set their hopes on other things and so make good the deficiency; for of all men they alone at once possess and hope for whatever they have conceived, so swift are they to set about whatever they have resolved.

§8 And all this they carry out amid toils and dangers, labouring their whole lives long; and they get but little enjoyment of what they possess, because they are always acquiring; they account no festival anything other than doing what is needful, and they count quiet inaction no less a misfortune than toilsome activity.

§9 So that if anyone were to gather all this together and say that they have been born never to have quiet themselves nor to allow it to other men, he would speak aright.

Chapter 71

§1 Yet against a city such as this, men of Lacedaemon, standing thus opposed to you, you still lose time, and you think that quiet is not what chiefly avails those men who in their preparations deal justly and who, if they are wronged, are plain for all to see that they will not submit; rather you mete out the even measure, on the principle of not harming the others and of defending yourselves so as not to be hurt.

§2 You would scarcely attain to that even if you dwelt beside a city like your own; as it is, as we have but now made plain, your ways are old-fashioned in your dealings with them.

§3 And necessity, as in a craft, makes what comes later always prevail; for a city at rest the customs that move nothing are the best, but for those who are driven to enter upon many things there is need also of much contrivance. For this reason the ways of the Athenians, through their abundant experience, have advanced further than yours.

§4 Up to this point, then, let your slowness be bounded; now help the rest and the Potidaeans, as you undertook, by marching at speed into Attica, so that you neither betray friends and kinsmen into the hands of your bitterest enemies, nor turn us, the rest of the allies, out of despair toward some other alliance.

§5 And we should be doing no wrong, not in the judgement of the gods of the oaths, nor in the eyes of men who perceive such things; for they break truces, not those who come to the aid of others in their isolation, but those who give no help to those with whom they have sworn together.

§6 If you are willing to be eager, we stand fast; for neither, if we changed, should we be doing what the gods sanction, nor should we find others with whom we are better acquainted than with you.

§7 Take good counsel upon these matters, and strive to hand down the Peloponnese no less than your fathers handed it down to you.

Chapter 72

§1 Such were the words of the Corinthians. Now an embassy of the Athenians, which had come before on other business, happened to be present in Lacedaemon, and when they became aware of what was being said, they judged that they must come forward before the Lacedaemonians, making no defence at all of the complaints which the cities were bringing, but making a declaration about the whole business — that they ought not to reach a decision quickly, but to examine it at greater length. And at the same time they wished to make plain how great a power their own city possessed, and to remind the elder men of what they knew and to give the younger an account of what they had no experience of, judging that by these arguments they would be turned more toward keeping quiet than toward making war.

§2 So they came before the Lacedaemonians and said that they too wished to speak to their assembly, if nothing prevented it. The Lacedaemonians bade them come forward, and the Athenians, coming forward, spoke as follows.

Chapter 73

§1 Our embassy did not come to bandy contradiction with your allies, but about the matters for which the city sent us; but becoming aware that no small outcry was being raised against us, we came forward — not to answer in turn to the complaints of the cities (for before you the arguments — neither ours nor theirs — would not be taking place before judges), but so that you may not, persuaded too readily by the allies in a great matter, deliberate worse; and at the same time because we wish to make clear, concerning the whole matter of accusation that stands against us, both that we possess what we possess not without reason, and that our city is worthy of account.

§2 And why need one speak of the utterly ancient things, of which the witness is hearsay rather than sight? But the Median war and all that you yourselves know — even if for those who put it forward it will always be something of a vexation — necessity compels us to speak of; for even when we were doing those deeds, the dangers we ran were run for the sake of the advantage, of which you had your share in the deed, while of the telling of it — if telling it be of any profit — we are not to be robbed altogether.

§3 It will be told not so much by way of seeking excuse as for witness, and to show you, should you not take good counsel, against what manner of city the struggle will stand.

§4 We assert that we were alone the first to run the hazard against the barbarian at Marathon, and that when he came the second time, not being able to make defence by land, we embarked with our whole people and joined in the sea-battle at Salamis — which prevented him from sailing against the Peloponnese and ravaging it city by city, since the cities would have been powerless to bring help to one another against a numerous fleet.

§5 And he himself supplied the greatest proof of this: for beaten in the ships, the power facing him being no longer like his own, he speedily withdrew with the greater part of his army.

Chapter 74

§1 This was the outcome, and it became clear that the Greeks' fate depended on their ships. We supplied the three most useful contributions: the largest number of ships, the ablest general and the most tireless commitment. Of the four hundred ships, ours numbered a little less than two-thirds. Our commander was Themistocles, who was chiefly responsible for the decision to fight in the straits, which clearly saved the situation. For this you yourselves honoured him more highly than any foreigner who had ever visited you.

§2 And zeal we showed, and of the most daring sort: for seeing that on land no one came to our help, while the rest were already enslaved as far as ourselves, we thought it right, leaving our city and letting our homes be destroyed, not even so to forsake the common cause of the remaining allies, nor, dispersed, to become useless to them, but to embark upon the ships and run the danger — and not to be angry with you because you did not before now avenge us.

§3 So we assert that we helped you no less than we received the help from you. For your part, you came to the rescue from cities that were still standing, and with a view to keeping for yourselves what was left, since you were afraid for the most part on your own account and not on ours (at the time when we were still safe you were not at hand); whereas we set out from a city that was no more, and, risking all on behalf of what lay in scant hope, saved both you — in part — and ourselves.

§4 And if we had earlier taken the side of the Mede out of fear for our land, as others did, or if we had later not had the courage to embark, as men already ruined, there would have been no need for you, possessing no sufficient ships, to fight a sea-battle at all; but everything would have gone forward for him in quiet, in what way he pleased.

Chapter 75

§1 Lacedaemonians, in view of our commitment then and the wisdom of our judgement, do we not deserve to be less bitterly resented by the Greeks for the empire we hold?

§2 For this very empire we took not by force, but because you were unwilling to stay on against what remained of the barbarian, while the allies themselves came to us and begged that we be appointed their commanders.

§3 And it was by the very facts themselves that we were compelled at the first to advance the empire to where it now stands — most of all by fear, then by honour, and last of all also by profit.

§4 It no longer seemed safe to risk giving up our empire: we were hated by many, some had already revolted and been subdued, and you were no longer the friends you had once been, but had become objects of suspicion and were at odds with us. Besides, those who revolted would have gone over to your side.

§5 For all men alike it is a thing no one grudges, when the greatest dangers are at stake, to weigh advantage well.

Chapter 76

§1 You at least, men of Lacedaemon, have brought the cities of the Peloponnese into a condition profitable to yourselves, and lead them; and if at that time you had stood fast throughout and incurred the same displeasure in the hegemony as we, we know well that you would have proved no less irksome to your allies, and would have been compelled either to rule with a strong hand or yourselves to run into danger.

§2 So we too have done nothing surprising or contrary to human nature in accepting an empire that was offered to us and refusing to give it up, overcome by the three strongest motives: honour, fear and advantage. Nor were we the first to act this way; the weaker have always been held down by the stronger. At the same time, we believed ourselves worthy of our position, and you agreed, until now, when calculations of your own advantage have led you to invoke justice. Yet no one who had the chance to gain something by force has ever put that argument first and refused to take more.

§3 And worthy of praise are they who, ruling over others in accordance with human nature, yet become more just than according to the power they possess.

§4 Others, we think, had they received the things which are ours, would display it best of all, if we practise moderation in any degree; whereas we, even out of our moderation, have gathered for the most part more unpopularity than praise — and not unreasonably.

Chapter 77

§1 For we are thought fond of lawsuits, even when we are worsted in the suits arising from our dealings with our allies, and when it is we ourselves who make the adjudications in our own city under the same statutes.

§2 And none of them reflects that in the case of those who elsewhere also hold rule, being even less moderate than we are towards their subjects, this is never cast in their teeth; for those to whom it lies open to use force have no need at all of giving judgment.

§3 But those who have been used to deal with us on a footing of equality, if they are worsted in any point beyond what they thought they ought to suffer — whether in judgement or through the power which the empire gives, and in any way whatsoever — do not feel gratitude that they are not robbed of more, but bear the shortcoming more grudgingly than if, laying the law aside from the first, we had openly made gain of them. Yet in that case they would not themselves have argued that the weaker ought not to give way to the stronger.

§4 Men, so it appears, are more enraged by being wronged than by being forced: for what comes from an equal seems overreaching, but what comes from a stronger, compulsion.

§5 At all events, under the Mede they endured things more dreadful than these; our empire, however, is thought hard to bear — and reasonably: for what is present is always a burden to those subject to it.

§6 As for you, if you should overthrow us and come to rule, you would speedily forfeit the goodwill which you have gained through fear of us — if, that is, you are of the sort you showed yourselves then, when you led against the Mede for but a little while: the same will you be found now also. For the usages you observe among yourselves admit of no mixing with the rest, and besides each one of you, going abroad, follows neither these usages nor those which the rest of Hellas observes.

Chapter 78

§1 Deliberate, then, slowly — the business before you is no small one — and do not, persuaded by judgements and complaints that belong to others, take upon yourselves a trouble of your own. Before you come to be in war, come to know beforehand its unreason — how great it is.

§2 For when it is drawn out long, war is wont for the most part to bring matters round upon chances, from which both sides hold equally aloof — and whichever way they will fall lies at hazard in darkness.

§3 Men, advancing into wars, lay hold first upon the deeds which they ought to have performed last; and when they are already in distress, then at last they take hold of words.

§4 As for us, neither being ourselves in any such error nor seeing you in it, we say to you — while for both alike good counsel is still a matter of free choice — not to dissolve the truce nor to violate the oaths, but to let the differences be settled by trial in accordance with the treaty. If not, we shall endeavour, calling the gods of the oaths to witness, to defend ourselves against you as men who have begun the war — in whatsoever way you shall point the way.

Chapter 79

§1 Such were the words of the Athenians. When the Lacedaemonians had heard both the complaints of the allies against the Athenians and what the Athenians themselves had said, they dismissed all the rest and deliberated among themselves alone upon the business before them.

§2 The views of the greater number inclined all one way — that the Athenians were already doing wrong, and that they must make war upon them with all speed. Then came forward Archidamus their king, a man reputed both intelligent and prudent, and he spoke as follows.

Chapter 80

§1 I too, men of Lacedaemon, am a man already experienced in many wars, and I see before me those of you who are of the same age with me — so that no one of us shall either long for this business out of inexperience, as the many would, or because he deems it a thing good and safe.

§2 You would find, then, that this business on which you are now deliberating is by no means one of the least, if a man reckon it out with sobriety.

§3 Against Peloponnesians and against our near neighbours our strength is much upon a par, and with speed it is possible to come to this point or that; but against men who possess land far away, who are besides the most experienced of all upon the sea, and are equipped beyond all others in everything else as well — in wealth both private and public, in ships, in horses, in arms, and in a multitude of men such as no other single place in Hellas possesses — and who hold moreover many subject allies that pay tribute: how should one lightly take up war against such men, and trusting in what, hasten to it unprepared?

§4 In ships? But there we are the weaker; and if we are to practise and fit out a counter-armament, time will come in. In money? But in this we are still far more deficient than in the ships: we have none in our common treasury, nor can it readily be brought from private sources.

Chapter 81

§1 One might perhaps be confident that we surpass them in arms and in numbers, so that we might march in, time after time, and lay their land waste.

§2 But they have another land, and much of it, which they rule; and the things of which they stand in need they will bring in from over the sea.

§3 And if in turn we set about winning their allies away, there will be need for us to bring help to these also with ships, the greater number of whom are islanders.

§4 What, then, will our war be? For unless we either prevail with our fleet or take away the revenues with which they feed their fleet, we shall be harmed in most respects.

§5 And in these circumstances it would no longer even be honourable for it to be brought to an end, above all if we shall be seen to have begun the difference rather than they.

§6 For let us not lift ourselves with that hope — that the war will speedily cease if we cut up their land; rather do I fear that we may leave it also to our children: so likely is it that the Athenians, by the proud spirit that is in them, will neither be enslaved to their land nor, like men without experience, be panic-stricken by the war.

Chapter 82

§1 Not that I bid you deal with them without perception — letting our allies be harmed, and, while they are plotting, failing to catch them at it; but not yet to move our arms, rather to send to them and bring our accusations, making plain neither too great a readiness for war, nor that we shall submit; and meanwhile to get ready the means that are our own, by the drawing-over of allies, Greeks and barbarians, if from any quarter we may receive an accession of power, either of ships or of money (and it is a thing that gives no handle for reproach, that all who, just as we do, are plotted against by the Athenians should take to themselves not Greeks only, but barbarians also, and so be saved); and at the same time let us make provision from our own means.

§2 And if in anything they comply with our embassies, that will be best; if not, then, once two or three years have gone by, we shall march against them, if it seem good, in better case, with our defences all secured.

§3 And perhaps, when they see our preparations already, and words answering to them, they will be the more inclined to yield — with their land still uncut, and deliberating over the good things they have at present, which are not yet destroyed.

§4 For do not suppose that you hold their land otherwise than as a hostage — and no less a hostage in proportion as it is the better tilled; land that must be spared as far as may be, and we must not, after driving the Athenians to desperation, have them thereafter so much the harder to take.

§5 For if, being unprepared, we make haste to cut it up in consequence of our allies' complaints, see to it that we do not, for the Peloponnese, act more disgracefully and with worse resource.

§6 For complaints, whether of cities or of private men, it is possible to settle; but a war, which we have all together taken up on behalf of private interests, and of which it is not in our power to know in what direction it will go, is not easy to end in a way that seems fair.

Chapter 83

§1 And that we, many as we are against a single city, do not speedily march upon it — let no one think that cowardice.

§2 For they too have allies not inferior, who bring in money; and war is not so much a matter of arms as of expenditure — it is through expenditure that arms are made of use — above all when landsmen are waging it against seamen.

§3 Let us provide the money first, then, and let us not be carried away before that by our allies' words; and let us quietly take thought in advance for whatever, of the things that will turn out, we shall in the main hold, upon either side of the cause.

Chapter 84

§1 Do not be ashamed of the slowness and hesitation for which we are most criticized. If you rush into action unprepared, you may take longer to bring the war to an end. Besides, we have always lived in a free and highly renowned city.

§2 And this above all may be clear-headed moderation: for we alone, owing to it, are not insolent in prosperity, and in adversity we yield less than others; and we take no uplift of pleasure from those who with praise goad us on towards dreadful things beyond our judgement; and if any man stirs us with an accusation, we are none the more persuaded for being provoked.

§3 Our discipline makes us both good soldiers and sound judges. It makes us good soldiers because a sense of shame is closely bound up with self-control, and courage with a sense of shame. We judge soundly because we are too little schooled to look down on the laws, and our strict training gives us too much self-control to disobey them. Nor are we excessively clever at useless things, criticizing the enemy's preparations in fine words and then failing to match those words in action. Instead, we assume that our neighbours' plans are much like our own, and that unforeseen events cannot be worked out by reasoning.

§4 Always we make ready in deed as against enemies who deliberate well; and our hopes are to depend not on their making mistakes, but on our providing securely for ourselves; and we are not to suppose that one man differs greatly from another, but that he is best who has been educated in the most necessary things.

Chapter 85

§1 These practices, then, which our fathers handed down to us, and which we, ever profiting by them, hold — these let us not let go, nor, hurried within a small fraction of a single day, let us deliberate over many lives and money and cities and reputation, but quietly — as is open to us more than to others, through our strength.

§2 And to the Athenians: send about Potidaea, and send about the things our allies say they are being wronged in — all the more since they themselves are ready to submit to trial; for against one who offers it, it is not lawful, before that, to proceed as against a wrongdoer. But make your preparations for the war at the same time. For so you will counsel best, and be most formidable to your opponents.

§3 Such were the words of Archidamus. Then, coming forward last, Sthenelaidas — one of the ephors at that time — addressed the Lacedaemonians as follows.

Chapter 86

§1 The many words of the Athenians I do not understand: they praised themselves on many points, but nowhere did they make answer to show that they are not wronging our allies and the Peloponnese; and yet, if they were good men then towards the Medes, but are bad men towards us now, they are worthy of a double penalty, because they have become bad instead of good.

§2 We, however, are the same now as we were then. If we are wise, we will not stand by while our allies are wronged, or delay coming to their defence: their suffering is already a fact, not just something still to come. [Note: the final clause is understood here as referring to the allies' present suffering. Another interpretation takes it as a threat that the Athenians will soon suffer.]

§3 For to others there belongs money in plenty, and ships, and horses; but to us there belong good allies — whom it is not for us to betray to the Athenians, nor to refer for decision to trials and words, men who are being wronged not in words: it is ours to take vengeance, and that with speed and with our whole strength.

§4 And let no one teach us that we, when we are wronged, ought to deliberate; it is the men who are going to do wrong who ought rather to deliberate, and for long.

§5 Vote, then, men of Lacedaemon, for war worthy of Sparta; suffer not the Athenians to grow greater, and let us not betray the allies outright, but with the gods let us march upon the wrongdoers.

Chapter 87

§1 Having spoken thus, he himself — being an ephor — submitted the matter to the vote in the assembly of the Lacedaemonians.

§2 But he (for it is by the shout and not by the ballot that they decide) said that he could not make out which of the two cries was the greater; and, being minded to impel them all the more towards war by having each declare his judgement in the open, he said: “Whichever of you, men of Lacedaemon, think the truce broken and the Athenians doing wrong, let him rise and take his stand in that place” — showing them a certain place — “whichever think otherwise, to the other side.” And rising, they divided;

§3 and those who thought that the truce had been broken proved by far the more numerous.

§4 And they summoned their allies and said that the Athenians appeared to them to be doing wrong, and that they wished to invite in all the allies and bring the question to the vote, so that, having deliberated in common, they might make the war, if it should seem good.

§5 They then withdrew home, having transacted this business; and the envoys of the Athenians later received their answer upon the business for which they had come.

§6 This decision of the assembly — that the truce should be broken — was reached in the fourteenth year of the thirty years' truce, which had been made after the Euboean affair and had now run so far.

Chapter 88

§1 The Lacedaemonians voted, then, that the truce had been broken and that the war must be made — not so much because they were persuaded by the words of their allies as because they feared the Athenians, lest they should grow to yet greater power, seeing that the greater part of Hellas was already subject to them.

Chapter 89

§1 For the Athenians advanced to the position in which they grew great in a manner such as this.

§2 When the Medes had withdrawn from Europe, having been defeated by the Greeks both by sea and by land, and when such of them as had fled to their ships at Mycale had been destroyed, Leotychides, king of the Lacedaemonians — the same who commanded the Greeks at Mycale — withdrew homewards, taking with him the allies from the Peloponnese; while the Athenians and the allies from Ionia and the Hellespont, by then in revolt from the King, stayed behind and besieged Sestus, which was held by the Medes; and pressing the siege through the winter, they took it, the barbarians having deserted it; and after that they sailed away out of the Hellespont, each to his own city.

§3 As for the Athenian public, as soon as the barbarians had gone out of their land, they at once brought home from the places where they had laid them away the children and the women and what remained of the household stock, and set about rebuilding the city and the walls; for of the circuit wall small portions only were standing, and of the houses many had been thrown down, while a few remained — those in which the great men of the Persians had themselves lodged.

Chapter 90

§1 The Lacedaemonians, becoming aware of what impended, came with an embassy. They would themselves have been better pleased to see neither the Athenians nor anyone else possessing walls; but for the most part it was their allies who pressed them on, in fear alike of the multitude of the Athenian fleet, which had not existed before, and of the daring that had been displayed in the Median war.

§2 They urged upon the Athenians not to fortify, but rather to demolish together with them the walls of all outside the Peloponnese that still stood; and they did not make clear to the Athenians either what they truly desired or the suspicion attaching to their design, but pressed the business of the barbarian — that, if he should come again, he would have no strong place anywhere from which to move, as at present from Thebes; and they said that for all men alike the Peloponnese was a sufficient place both of retreat and of sallying out.

§3 And the Athenians, on the advice of Themistocles, answered the Lacedaemonians, when they had said these things, that they would send envoys to them about the matters they spoke of, and immediately dismissed them. Themistocles urged that he himself be dispatched to Lacedaemon with all speed; and that the fellow-envoys chosen to go with him should not be sent off immediately, but should hold back, until the wall should have been raised high enough to fight from, at the most necessary height; and that all in the city together — themselves, and the women, and the children — should fortify, sparing no building, private or public, from which any help could come to the work, but pulling down everything.

§4 And having given them these instructions, and having promised the rest besides — namely that he himself would manage the business over there — he departed.

§5 And having come to Lacedaemon, he did not go near the authorities, but passed his time and made excuses; and whenever one of those in office asked him why he did not come before the public, he would say that he was waiting for his fellow-envoys, who had been left behind because of some business, but that he expected them to come shortly — and that he wondered that they were not yet there.

Chapter 91

§1 When they heard this, they were persuaded by Themistocles because of their friendship for him; but as others kept arriving and bringing a plain accusation that walls were being built and were already rising to a height, they had no means of disbelieving it.

§2 When he learned of this, he told them not to let themselves be carried away by words rather than sending men of their own — reliable men, who would examine and report faithfully.

§3 So they sent the men off; and Themistocles sent secret word to the Athenians about them, bidding them detain them with the least possible display and not let them go until they themselves had come back (for by now his fellow-envoys had reached him — Habronichus son of Lysicles and Aristides son of Lysimachus — announcing that the wall was already sufficient); for he feared that the Lacedaemonians, once they had heard a plain account, would no longer let them go.

§4 The Athenians, then, just as word had been sent, held the envoys under guard; and Themistocles came before the Lacedaemonians and said openly at last that their city was now walled so as to be sufficient to save those who dwelt in it; and that if the Lacedaemonians or the allies wished to come to them on an embassy, they would be coming to men who would henceforth weigh both their own advantage and the common interest.

§5 For when it seemed better, they said, to abandon their city and go aboard the ships, they had ventured to do so on their own resolve and without them; and in whatever deliberations were taken in common with them they had shown themselves behind none in judgement.

§6 That it therefore seemed best to them now also that their own city should have a wall; and that this would be more advantageous both for the citizens in their private interest and for all the allies;

§7 for without an armament to match it was impossible to deliberate on any like or equal terms about the common interest. Either, he said, all must fight on as allies unwalled, or they must accept that this too was rightly done.

Chapter 92

§1 When they heard this, the Lacedaemonians showed no open anger towards the Athenians (for they had gone on their embassy not to obstruct, but, on their own showing, to offer advice in the common interest; and at that time, besides, the Athenians stood highest in their favour because of their zeal against the Mede), but, having failed of their purpose, they were vexed in secret; and the envoys of both sides went home without complaint.

Chapter 93

§1 In this way the Athenians walled their city in a short time.

§2 The construction still shows clearly that the work was done in haste. The foundations consist of stones of every kind, in places not even fitted together, but laid as each happened to be brought in. Many gravestones and worked stones were built into them. The city's circuit was extended outwards in every direction, so in their haste they put every available material to use.

§3 Themistocles also persuaded them to build the rest of the Piraeus (the work had been begun before, in his year of office, in which he was archon of the Athenians for the annual term), judging the place a fine one, with three harbours formed by nature, and judging that, now that the Athenians themselves had become a seafaring people, it would bring them a great advantage towards the

§4 acquisition of power. He was the first who dared to say that they must hold on to the sea, and from the outset he was helping to lay the foundations of their empire.

§5 And on his advice they built the thickness of wall that is still plain to see about the Piraeus: two carts, facing one another, would bring up their stones abreast. Inside there was neither rubble nor clay, but great stones fitted together, jointed by angle-cuttings, and bound to one another on the outer face with iron and lead. Its height, however, was completed to only about half of what he had designed.

§6 For by its size and its thickness he wished to ward off the attempts of enemies, and he considered that for the guard few men would suffice, and those of the most useless sort, while all the others would go aboard the ships.

§7 For he clung most of all to the ships, having observed — as it seems to me — that the king's army found the approach by sea easier than that by land; and he deemed the Piraeus more serviceable than the upper city, and he often advised the Athenians that, should they ever be worsted by land, they must go down to the Piraeus and with their ships offer resistance to all.

§8 So the Athenians were walled in this manner, and their other preparations were put in hand at once, after the withdrawal of the Medes.

Chapter 94

§1 Pausanias son of Cleombrotus, a Lacedaemonian, was sent out as commander of the Greeks with twenty ships from the Peloponnese; and the Athenians, with thirty ships, and a great contingent of the other allies sailed in his company.

§2 They made a campaign against Cyprus and subdued the greater part of it; and later against Byzantium, which the Medes held; and in this command they brought that siege to its end.

Chapter 95

§1 When his violence was now plain to see, the rest of the Greeks were vexed — and not least the Ionians and all who had lately been set free from the king — and, coming frequently to the Athenians, they urged the Athenians to become their commanders in virtue of their kinship, and not to give way to Pausanias wherever he should attempt violence.

§2 The Athenians accepted what was urged and gave it their attention, as men who were not going to stand by and overlook matters, and were going to settle everything else in whatever way should appear best to them.

§3 In the meantime the Lacedaemonians sent for Pausanias, examining him about the things reported; for much wrongdoing was being brought against him in accusation by the Greeks who kept arriving, and his conduct showed itself more as an imitation of tyranny than as a command.

§4 And it fell out at the same time both that he was called home and that the allies, out of hatred for him, went over to the Athenians — all except the soldiers from the Peloponnese.

§5 On coming to Lacedaemon he was called to account for certain private wrongs against individuals; from the gravest charges he was acquitted of any wrongdoing; Medism was far from the least of the accusations brought against him, and this appeared the clearest of all.

§6 Him they no longer sent out in command; Dorcis and certain others they sent with him, with an army that was not large; and to these the allies no longer granted the hegemony.

§7 These, becoming aware of it, went away; and the Lacedaemonians sent out no others afterwards, fearing that those sent out might become worse, as they had already seen happen in the case of Pausanias; and, being minded to be quit of the Median war, they considered the Athenians well able to conduct it, and convenient for themselves at the time then present.

Chapter 96

§1 The Athenians, having received the hegemony in this way with the willing consent of the allies because of their hatred of Pausanias, assessed which of the cities were to furnish money against the barbarian, and which were to furnish ships; the pretext was, by ravaging the king's country, to take vengeance for what they had suffered.

§2 And the office of the Hellenotamiae was then established for the Athenians for the first time — these were the men who received the tribute; for this was the name given to the bringing in of the money. The first tribute was assessed at four hundred and sixty talents. Delos was their treasury, and the congresses were held at the sanctuary.

Chapter 97

§1 While at the first they commanded allies who were autonomous and deliberated from common congresses, so much came upon them in the way of wars and settlements of affairs between this war and the Median war — all that befell them against the barbarian, and against their own allies when these rose in revolt, and against the Peloponnesians as they fell in with them on each occasion.

§2 These things I have written down, and I have made the digression in my narrative for this reason: because to all who came before me this ground was left untouched, and they compiled either the Hellenic affairs before the Median war or the Median war itself; of whom the one man who also touched upon this in his Attic History was Hellanicus, and he made mention of it briefly, and not with exactness about the dates. At the same time it serves as a demonstration of the rule of the Athenians, of the way in which it came to be.

Chapter 98

§1 First, they took Eion on the Strymon, which the Medes held, by siege, and enslaved it, Cimon son of Miltiades being general.

§2 Then Scyros, the island in the Aegean, which the Dolopians inhabited, they enslaved, and colonized it themselves.

§3 Against the Carystians, apart from the other Euboeans, a war befell them; and in course of time they came to terms by agreement.

§4 Against the Naxians, who had revolted after these things, they made war and pressed them with a siege; this was the first allied city to be enslaved contrary to the established order, and afterwards the rest, each as it chanced.

Chapter 99

§1 There were other causes besides of the revolts, and the chief were the exactions of tribute and of ships, and desertion, wherever it occurred; for the Athenians enforced collection with strictness and were oppressive, bringing the necessity of enduring toil upon men who were not accustomed to it and not willing.

§2 And in other ways too, the Athenians were somehow no longer equally pleasing as rulers, nor did they any longer campaign on equal terms; and it was easy for them to bring the revolters back into subjection.

§3 Of these things the allies themselves became the causes; for out of this very weariness of the campaigns most of them, that they might not be away from home, assessed themselves to pay money instead of ships, the expense as it accrued; and the Athenians' fleet grew great from the outlay which those allies contributed, while they themselves, whenever they revolted, entered the war unprepared and without experience.

Chapter 100

§1 After these things came also the battle on land and the sea-battle at the river Eurymedon in Pamphylia, fought by the Athenians and their allies against the Medes; and on the same day the Athenians, with Cimon son of Miltiades as general, were victorious in both; and they captured Phoenician triremes and destroyed them all, to the number of two hundred.

§2 Some time later it came about that the Thasians revolted from them, having come to a difference over the trading-posts on the coast of Thrace opposite and over the mine which they held; and the Athenians, sailing against Thasos with their ships, prevailed in a sea-battle and disembarked upon the land,

§3 and at about the same time, sending to the Strymon ten thousand settlers of their own and of the allies, to found what were then called the Nine Ways and are now called Amphipolis, they themselves took possession of the Nine Ways, which the Edonians held; but, advancing up-country into the interior of Thrace, they were destroyed at Drabescus, in the Edonian country, by all the Thracians together, to whom the founding of this place, the Nine Ways, was a thing of enmity.

Chapter 101

§1 The Thasians, beaten in battle and now under siege, called upon the Lacedaemonians, and kept urging them to come to their aid by making an invasion of Attica.

§2 The Lacedaemonians promised, in secret from the Athenians, and were on the point of doing it; but they were prevented by the earthquake that occurred, during which their Helots — with the Thuriatae and the Aethaeans of the perioeci — also revolted to Ithome. Most of the Helots were descendants of the Messenians of old, who had been enslaved at that time; wherefore all alike came to be called Messenians.

§3 The war against those in Ithome had now taken its fixed course for the Lacedaemonians; and the Thasians, in the third year of their siege, came to terms with the Athenians — to raze their wall, to hand over their ships, to fix whatever money they were due to repay and pay it down at once, and to pay for the future, and to relinquish the mainland and the mine.

Chapter 102

§1 The Lacedaemonians, as the war against the men in Ithome lengthened out for them, summoned their other allies besides, and the Athenians as well; and the Athenians came, with Cimon as general, in a force by no means small.

§2 It was above all the Athenians whom they summoned, because they were believed to be good at fighting against walls; whereas they themselves, the siege being now of long standing, seemed to be wanting in this; for they would have taken the place by main force.

§3 From this campaign the first open difference between the Lacedaemonians and the Athenians arose. For the Lacedaemonians, since the position was not being taken by force, fearing the boldness of the Athenians and their innovating temper, and judging them at the same time to be of another stock — in case, if they stayed, they should be persuaded by the men in Ithome and stir up a rising — sent them away alone of the allies, not disclosing the suspicion, but saying that they no longer had need of them.

§4 The Athenians were aware that they were not being sent away upon the better ground, but because some suspicion had been entertained; and taking it ill, and refusing to brook such treatment at the hands of the Lacedaemonians, immediately upon their withdrawal they abandoned the alliance that had been made with them against the Mede, and allied themselves with the Argives, the enemies of the Lacedaemonians; and with the Thessalians at the same time the same oaths and alliance were established with both alike.

Chapter 103

§1 In the tenth year the men in Ithome, no longer able to hold out, came to terms with the Lacedaemonians on condition of marching out from the Peloponnese under a truce and never again setting foot on it; and should anyone be caught, he was to be the slave of him that took him.

§2 There had also been, some time before, a Pythian oracle for the Lacedaemonians, bidding them let go the suppliant of Zeus of Ithome.

§3 They themselves then came out, and children and women; and the Athenians, their enmity towards the Lacedaemonians being by now established, received them and settled them at Naupactus, which they had but lately captured, the Ozolian Locrians being then in possession of it.

§4 The Megarians also revolted from the Lacedaemonians and went over into alliance with the Athenians, because the Corinthians were pressing them hard in a war over the boundaries of the land; and the Athenians took Megara and Pegae, built for the Megarians the long walls from the city down to Nisaea, and themselves put garrisons in them. And from this time the fierce hatred first began to arise against the Athenians, not least of all among the Corinthians.

Chapter 104

§1 Inarus son of Psammetichus — a Libyan, king of the Libyans bordering on Egypt — who started out from Mareia, the city above Pharos, brought the greater part of Egypt into revolt from King Artaxerxes; and, having himself become ruler, he drew in the Athenians.

§2 They came away, leaving Cyprus — for they happened to be campaigning against Cyprus with two hundred ships, their own and their allies’ — and, sailing up from the sea into the Nile, masters as they were of the river and of two-thirds of Memphis, they carried the war against the third part, the one called the White Wall. Within it were those of the Persians and Medes who had fled for refuge there, and those of the Egyptians who had not joined the revolt.

Chapter 105

§1 The Athenians, disembarking from their ships at Halieis, fought a battle against Corinthians and Epidaurians, and the Corinthians were victorious. Later on the Athenians fought a sea-battle off Cecryphalia against ships of the Peloponnesians, and the Athenians were victorious.

§2 War with the Aeginetans having afterwards arisen for the Athenians, a great sea-battle was fought off Aegina between Athenians and Aeginetans, and allies were present on both sides; and the Athenians were victorious and, taking seventy ships of theirs, disembarked upon the land and laid siege, Leocrates son of Stroebus being general.

§3 Thereupon the Peloponnesians, wishing to bring help to the Aeginetans, first ferried across into Aegina three hundred hoplites, reinforcements of Corinthians and Epidaurians; next they seized the heights of Geraneia, and the Corinthians with their allies came down into the Megarid, supposing that the Athenians would be unable to bring help to the Megarians, with much of their army away, both at Aegina and in Egypt; and that, if they brought help even so, they would have to withdraw from Aegina.

§4 The Athenians did not stir the army before Aegina; but of the men left in the city the very oldest and the very youngest came to Megara, Myronides commanding.

§5 In the battle that was joined with the Corinthians the issue hung evenly; the two sides separated from one another, and each supposed that it came off no worse in the engagement.

§6 The Athenians — for after all they had somewhat the better of it — set up a trophy upon the departure of the Corinthians; the Corinthians, taunted by the elders in the city, made ready, and after an interval of about twelve days came and set up a trophy themselves, as victors; and the Athenians, coming out from Megara to the rescue, destroyed the men who were setting up the trophy, and, engaging with the rest, prevailed.

Chapter 106

§1 The beaten men fell back, and a portion of them, by no means a small one, hard pressed and missing their road, plunged into a certain enclosed property belonging to a private man, which chanced to have a great trench encompassing it, and there was no way out.

§2 The Athenians, learning of it, shut them off in front with their hoplites, and, having stationed their light-armed men round them in a circle, shot down with missiles all who had entered; and this proved a great disaster for the Corinthians. The mass of the army withdrew home.

Chapter 107

§1 At about this time the Athenians began to build their long walls also down to the sea — the one to Phalerum and the one to the Piraeus.

§2 The Phocians invaded Doris, the mother-city of the Lacedaemonians — Boium, Cytinium and Erineum — and captured one of these small towns. The Lacedaemonians came to the help of the Dorians with one thousand five hundred hoplites of their own and ten thousand of their allies, Nicomedes son of Cleombrotus commanding in place of the king Pleistoanax son of Pausanias, who was still a child; and having constrained the Phocians by an agreement to give back the city, they were withdrawing home again.

§3 By sea, if they wished to make the crossing through the Crisaean gulf, the Athenians were on the point of stopping them, sailing round with their fleet; and by way of the Geraneia it did not seem safe for them to march, with the Athenians holding Megara and Pegae — for the Geraneia is a road difficult of access, and it was always kept garrisoned by the Athenians, and at the time they were aware that the Athenians were on the point of barring that route also.

§4 It seemed best to them, waiting in Boeotia, to consider by what route they would get through most safely. And certain men among the Athenians were drawing them on in secret as well, hoping to put a stop both to the democracy and to the building of the long walls.

§5 The Athenians marched out against them with their whole strength, along with one thousand Argives and with the rest of the allies, each according to their means; in all they came to fourteen thousand.

§6 Judging that they had no way to get through, they marched against them, with some suspicion also of an overthrow of the democracy.

§7 There also came to help the Athenians, in accordance with the alliance, horsemen of the Thessalians; and these went over in the engagement to the Lacedaemonians.

Chapter 108

§1 A battle was joined at Tanagra in Boeotia; the Lacedaemonians and their allies were victorious, and great was the slaughter on both sides.

§2 The Lacedaemonians, after marching into the Megarid and cutting down the trees, went home again by way of the Geraneia and the Isthmus; and the Athenians, on the sixty-second day after the battle, marched into Boeotia, Myronides commanding,

§3 and, having defeated the Boeotians in battle at Oenophyta, they made themselves masters of all the country of Boeotia and of Phocis, razed the wall of the Tanagraeans, and took for hostages from the Opuntian Locrians a hundred of the richest men; and their own long walls they brought to completion.

§4 The Aeginetans also, after this, came to terms with the Athenians, razing their walls and handing over their ships, and assessing a tribute for the time to come.

§5 And the Athenians, with Tolmides son of Tolmaeus as general, sailed round the Peloponnese; and they burned the dockyard of the Lacedaemonians, and took Chalcis, a city belonging to the Corinthians, and prevailed over the Sicyonians in a battle upon disembarking on the land.

Chapter 109

§1 In Egypt the Athenians and the allies held out; and many kinds of war arose for them.

§2 For at first the Athenians were masters of Egypt; and the King sent to Lacedaemon Megabazus, a Persian, with money, so that, once the Peloponnesians had been persuaded to invade Attica, he might lead the Athenians away out of Egypt.

§3 But when the business made no progress for him and the money was being consumed in vain, the rest of the money was carried back with Megabazus into Asia; and the King sent Megabyzus son of Zopyrus, a Persian, with a great army.

§4 He, on his arrival by land, prevailed over the Egyptians and their allies in battle, and drove the Greeks out of Memphis; and at last he shut them up in the island of Prosopitis and besieged them there one year and six months, until, having drained the canal and turned the water aside elsewhere, he set the ships upon dry land and made the greater part of the island mainland; and crossing over on foot he took the island.

Chapter 110

§1 In this way, after fighting for six years, the affairs of the Greeks came to destruction; and few out of the many, marching through Libya, found safety at Cyrene; the majority perished.

§2 And Egypt again became subject to the King, except for Amyrtaeus, the king in the marshes; him they were unable to capture, because of the size of the marsh, and because, at the same time, the marshmen are the most warlike of the Egyptians.

§3 Inarus the king of the Libyans, who had brought about the whole business concerning Egypt, was taken by treachery and crucified.

§4 And fifty triremes put out in relief from Athens and from the rest of the alliance, sailing for Egypt, and occupied the Mendesian mouth, with no knowledge at all of the things that had happened; and upon them fell an attack of infantry from the land and from the sea the fleet of the Phoenicians, and these destroyed the greater part of the ships; and the few got away home again.

§5 So came to an end the great expedition of the Athenians and of the allies into Egypt.

Chapter 111

§1 From Thessaly, Orestes son of Echecratides, king of the Thessalians, being in exile, persuaded the Athenians to restore him; and the Athenians took with them the Boeotians and the Phocians, who were their allies, and made an expedition into Thessaly against Pharsalus. They were masters of such of the land as they passed over without ranging far from the main body (for the horsemen of the Thessalians kept them back), but they did not take the city, and nothing else of that for which they had marched made any progress for them; and they withdrew again, unsuccessful, taking Orestes with them.

§2 After this, not long afterwards, a thousand Athenians, having embarked on the ships that were at Pegae (for they themselves held Pegae), sailed along the coast to Sicyon, Pericles son of Xanthippus being general; and disembarking, they defeated in battle such of the Sicyonians as came to close quarters with them.

§3 And at once, taking the Achaeans with them, and sailing across past Acarnania, they made an expedition against Oeniadae and besieged it; they did not take it, however, but withdrew home.

Chapter 112

§1 Later, after an interval of three years, a truce for five years was concluded between the Peloponnesians and the Athenians.

§2 And the Athenians held aloof from war with the Greeks, but with two hundred ships of their own and of the allies they were campaigning against Cyprus, Cimon being general.

§3 And sixty of these ships sailed to Egypt at the summons of Amyrtaeus, the king in the marshes, while the rest were besieging Citium.

§4 Upon the death of Cimon, and when a famine had set in, they withdrew from Citium, and, sailing past Salamis in Cyprus, they fought a battle at sea and a battle on land at the same time against the Phoenicians and the Cyprians and the Cilicians; and, victorious in both, they returned home, and with them the ships that had come back from Egypt.

§5 The Lacedaemonians after these events took the field in the war which is called the Sacred War, and, having become masters of the sanctuary at Delphi, they handed it over to the Delphians; and again later the Athenians, when the Lacedaemonians had withdrawn, marched, and, having mastered it, handed it over to the Phocians.

Chapter 113

§1 And when some time had passed after these events, the Athenians — the exiled Boeotians being in possession of Orchomenus and Chaeronea and certain other places of Boeotia — made an expedition with a thousand hoplites of their own and such forces of the allies as each supplied, against these places, which were hostile, Tolmides son of Tolmaeus being general; and, taking Chaeronea and enslaving it, they withdrew, having established a garrison.

§2 And as they were on their way back, the Boeotian exiles from Orchomenus set upon them at Coronea, together with Locrians and Euboean exiles and all who were of the same mind; and, prevailing in the battle, they destroyed some of the Athenians and took the others alive.

§3 And the Athenians abandoned the whole of Boeotia, having made a truce on condition that they should recover their men.

§4 And the exiled Boeotians came home, and they and all the others alike became autonomous again as before.

Chapter 114

§1 Not long after these events Euboea revolted from the Athenians; and when Pericles had already crossed over to it with an Athenian army, word was brought to him that Megara had revolted, that the Peloponnesians were on the point of invading Attica, and that the garrisons of the Athenians had been destroyed by the Megarians, except such as escaped to Nisaea; and the Megarians, having brought in Corinthians and Sicyonians and Epidaurians, had revolted. Pericles then was bringing the army back again with all speed out of Euboea.

§2 And after this the Peloponnesians marched into Attica, upon Eleusis and Thria, and ravaged the land, Pleistoanax son of Pausanias, king of the Lacedaemonians, being their commander; and going no farther onwards, they withdrew home.

§3 And the Athenians, crossing over again to Euboea under the generalship of Pericles, subdued the whole of it; and they brought the rest under control by agreement, but Histiaea they emptied of its inhabitants and themselves held its land.

Chapter 115

§1 Having withdrawn from Euboea, not long after they made a truce for thirty years with the Lacedaemonians and their allies, giving back Nisaea and Pegae and Troezen and Achaea; for these belonged to the Peloponnesians, though the Athenians held them.

§2 In the sixth year a war arose between the Samians and the Milesians concerning Priene, and the Milesians, who were getting the worse of it in the war, came to the Athenians crying out loudly against the Samians; and private men from Samos itself likewise took the matter up with them, wanting to revolutionize the constitution.

§3 The Athenians accordingly sailed to Samos with forty ships and established a democracy there; and they took hostages of the Samians, fifty boys and as many men, and lodged them in Lemnos, and, leaving a garrison behind, sailed away.

§4 For there were some of the Samians who did not submit, but fled to the mainland; and these, having made a compact with the most powerful men in the city and an alliance with Pissuthnes son of Hystaspes, who then held Sardis, and having collected mercenaries to the number of seven hundred, crossed over by night to Samos,

§5 and first they rose against the common people and got the better of most of them; then, having spirited their hostages away out of Lemnos, they revolted, and handed over to Pissuthnes the garrison of the Athenians and the magistrates who were present with them, and were at once preparing to march against Miletus. The Byzantines revolted together with them.

Chapter 116

§1 When the Athenians heard this, they sailed against Samos with sixty ships. Sixteen of these took no part in the battle: some had gone to Caria to watch for the Phoenician fleet, and others to Chios and Lesbos to call for help. With the remaining forty-four ships, under Pericles and nine fellow generals, they fought off the island of Tragia against seventy Samian ships, twenty of them troop transports. All the Samian ships were returning from Miletus. The Athenians won the battle.

§2 Later forty ships from Athens and twenty-five of the Chians and Lesbians came to their aid; and, disembarking and having the upper hand on land, they besieged the town with three walls, and at the same time by sea.

§3 And Pericles, taking sixty ships from those riding at anchor, departed with all speed for Caunus and Caria, word having been brought that Phoenician ships were sailing against them; for from Samos, too, Stesagoras and others had gone off with five ships against the Phoenicians.

Chapter 117

§1 Meanwhile the Samians, having suddenly sailed out and fallen upon the unfenced camp, destroyed the ships on outpost duty, and, in the battle with the ships that put out to meet them, they were victorious; and they held command of the sea off their own coast for about fourteen days, bringing in and carrying out whatsoever they wished.

§2 And when Pericles came back with the fleet, they were again shut up. Later there came as help from Athens forty ships under Thucydides and Hagnon and Phormio, and twenty under Tlepolemus and Anticles, and of the Chians and Lesbians thirty.

§3 And the Samians fought one brief sea-battle; but, being unable to hold out, they were forced out by the investment in the ninth month and submitted on terms: razing their wall, giving hostages, surrendering their ships, and undertaking to repay the money that had been spent, by instalments at fixed times. And the Byzantines likewise came in, to be subjects as they had been before.

Chapter 118

§1 After these events there came to pass, not many years later, the things that had been related before — the affairs of Corcyra and the affairs of Potidaea — and all that became the ground of this war.

§2 All these things, whatsoever the Greeks did against one another and against the barbarian, came to pass within about fifty years, between the retreat of Xerxes and the beginning of this war. In these years the Athenians brought their empire under firmer control and themselves advanced to great power; and the Lacedaemonians, aware of it, did not stand in the way except for a little, and were quiet for the greater part of the time — for even before this they were not quick to go to war unless compelled, and were also in some measure hindered by wars of their own — until the power of the Athenians was clearly on the rise and they began to lay hands on their own alliance. Then they no longer held it endurable, but, having taken up this war, they judged that the attempt must be made with all eagerness, and the power brought down, if they could.

§3 Among the Lacedaemonians themselves it had been decided that the truce had been broken and that the Athenians were in the wrong; and they sent to Delphi and asked the god whether it would be better for them to make war; and he answered them, as the story goes, that if they fought with all their might they would have the victory, and that he himself would help them, whether they called on him or unbidden.

Chapter 119

§1 Again, upon summoning the allies, they wished to bring in a vote on whether it was necessary to make war. And when the ambassadors from the alliance had come and a congress had been held, the others spoke as each wished, the majority making accusations against the Athenians and demanding that the war be undertaken; and the Corinthians — who had already made entreaty beforehand, from city to city, to each in private, to vote for the war, fearing for Potidaea, lest it be destroyed beforehand — were present on this occasion as well, and, coming forward last, spoke as follows.

Chapter 120

§1 Fellow allies, we can no longer accuse the Lacedaemonians of having failed either to vote for war themselves or to assemble us for that purpose. For leaders must look ahead to the common interest while attending to their own interests on equal terms with everyone else, just as they receive special honour from everyone in other matters.

§2 Those of us who have already had dealings with the Athenians need no lesson in guarding against them. But those who live further inland, away from the sea, must understand that unless they defend the coastal communities, they will find it harder to bring their harvests down to the coast and obtain in return the goods that the sea supplies to the mainland. They must not misjudge what is being said now as irrelevant to them. They should expect that, if they abandon the coast, the danger may one day reach them too, and recognize that their own interests are equally at stake in these deliberations.

§3 That is why they must not hesitate to exchange peace for war. Sensible men remain at peace when they are not being wronged; brave men go to war when they are wronged, and make peace again when a favourable opportunity arises. They must neither let success in war go to their heads nor accept injustice out of love for the quiet of peace.

§4 For he who hesitates for the sake of pleasure would most quickly be robbed of the very delight of his ease — that for the sake of which he hesitates — were he to keep quiet; and he who abounds in success in war gives no thought, being lifted up by a boldness that is not to be trusted.

§5 For many things, ill considered though they were, have yet come to a good end because those they met with were still more without counsel; and still more things that seemed well deliberated have turned out the opposite, to a shameful issue. For no one pursues in execution in equal measure what he had purposed in his confidence; but we judge with an eye to what is safe, and in the deed itself we fall short through fear.

Chapter 121

§1 As for us, we are now raising the war as men who are wronged, and with complaints enough to our hand; and when we have defended ourselves against the Athenians, we shall lay it to rest in season.

§2 In many respects it is likely that we shall prevail — first, as men who excel in numbers and in experience of war, and then as men who all alike go wherever they are ordered;

§3 a fleet too, the thing with which they are strong, we shall furnish out of the substance that each of us already has, and out of the moneys at Delphi and Olympia; for by raising a loan we are able to engage their foreign sailors for a greater wage. For the force of the Athenians is a hired one rather than their own; ours would suffer this less, getting the greater part of its strength from men’s bodies rather than from money.

§4 By one victory in a sea-battle, in all likelihood, they are caught; and if they should hold out, we too will practise our naval craft over a longer time, and when we have brought our skill to an even level, we shall surpass them at least in courage. For the good thing that we have by nature could not come to them by teaching; while that in which they excel by skill we can pull down by practice.

§5 And money, so as to have it for these ends, we will supply; or else it would be a terrible thing if the allies of those men, paying on behalf of their own slavery, do not refuse, while we — looking to punish our enemies and at the same time to save ourselves, and looking not to have it taken away from us by those men and so to suffer harm at their hands over these very resources — should not spend it.

Chapter 122

§1 There are other ways of war open to us as well: the revolt of their allies, above all the cutting away of the revenues by which they are strong, and the counter-fortification of their land, and other things besides that no one could now foresee. Least of all things does war proceed upon terms laid down beforehand; war itself for the most part devises its measures to meet whatever chance presents; and in it the man who handles it, keeping his temper entire, is the more secure, while the man whose anger is roused over it stumbles no less.

§2 And let us bear in mind, too, that if the differences were for each of us with opponents over boundaries of land, they would be bearable; but as it is the Athenians are a match for the whole of us together, and still more powerful city by city, so that unless we defend ourselves against them in concert — by whole nations, and each several city — with one judgement, they will make an easy conquest of us when we are divided. And let him know that defeat, dreadful though it be for anyone to hear of, brings nothing else than downright slavery.

§3 A thing shameful even to speak of in words — for the Peloponnese, and for so many cities to be in misery under one. In such a case we should be seen either to be suffering justly, or to be submitting through cowardice and to appear baser than our fathers, who set Hellas free — while we do not make her freedom fast even for ourselves, and we permit a city to be set up as tyrant, we who think it our part to put down absolute rulers in a single city.

§4 And we do not know in what way these things of ours are clear of the three greatest of misfortunes: lack of understanding, or softness, or carelessness. For you, far from having escaped them, have turned aside to that contempt which has done most mischief to most, and which, because it has been the trip of many, has had its name changed to the opposite — want of sense.

Chapter 123

§1 As for the things that have happened before, why need one press them as accusations longer than serves the present? But as for the things that are going to come hereafter, you must submit to the toil of helping those present — for it is your ancestral way to gain your virtues through toils — and you must not change your habit now that you have come to excel a little in wealth and in command of means — for it is not right that what was acquired through scarcity should be lost through abundance — but must go to the war emboldened on many grounds, with the god having granted his oracle and himself promised to help, and with the whole rest of Hellas joining in the struggle at your side, some from fear, some from advantage.

§2 And you will not be the first to break the truce — the truce which even the god, in bidding you make war, counts it transgression to break — but to those who have been wronged you will come with help rather. For it is not those who defend themselves that break truces, but those who are the first to come on.

Chapter 124

§1 So, since it is well for you from every side to make war, and we are advising you in this as one body — if indeed that is the surest of all, that the same things advantage cities and private men alike — do not delay to make redress for the Potidaeans, who are Dorians and are being besieged by Ionians, which formerly was the other way about, nor to pursue the freedom of the rest; for it is no longer possible, while we wait, that some should already be harmed, and that the others — should we be known as having come together, to be sure, yet as not daring to defend ourselves — should not suffer the same thing much later;

§2 But judging that you are come to a point of necessity, men of the alliance, and that at the same time these are the best things that can be said, vote for the war — not in fear of the immediate terror, but in desire for the peace that will come of it for the longer term; for out of war peace is the better assured, while out of quiet the not making of war is not equally without danger.

§3 And judging that the city now established in Hellas has been set up as tyrant over all alike, so that of the others it rules some already and has the rest in mind, let us forestall it by marching against it, and let us live ourselves henceforth out of danger, and free the Greeks who are now enslaved. Such were the words of the Corinthians.

Chapter 125

§1 The Lacedaemonians, when they had heard the opinion of all, put the vote to every one of the allies who was present, each in its turn, to the greater city and to the lesser alike; and the majority voted to make war.

§2 The war having been voted, it was impossible for them to undertake it at once, since they were unprovided; it was decided, rather, that each should furnish what was fitting for him, and that there be no putting off. Yet, while they were getting ready what was needed, not a full year was spent, but less, before they invaded Attica and undertook the war openly.

Chapter 126

§1 Meanwhile, in this interval, embassies kept going to and fro to the Athenians, lodging complaints, in order that, if the Athenians should give ear to nothing, the ground for making war might be as great as possible.

§2 And first the Lacedaemonians, having sent ambassadors, bade the Athenians drive out the curse of the goddess; and the curse was this.

§3 Cylon was an Athenian, an Olympic victor of an earlier day, well born and powerful; and he had married a daughter of Theagenes, a man of Megara, who at that time was tyrant of Megara.

§4 When Cylon consulted the god at Delphi, the god enjoined on him to seize the acropolis of the Athenians at the greatest festival of Zeus.

§5 He, having taken a force from Theagenes and persuaded his friends, when the Olympic games in the Peloponnese had come round, seized the acropolis with intent to make himself tyrant, deeming the festival to be the greatest festival of Zeus, and deeming that something was owed to himself as a victor in the Olympic games.

§6 Whether the greatest festival had been meant in Attica or anywhere else he no longer considered, nor did the oracle make it clear — for the Athenians have also the Diasia, which is called the greatest festival of Zeus Meilichius outside the city, at which they sacrifice in full force many things that are not victims but hallowed offerings of the country — and thinking that he understood aright he attempted the deed.

§7 The Athenians, becoming aware of it, came to the rescue in full force from the fields, and sitting down before the place they besieged it.

§8 As time went on, most of the Athenians, worn out by their sitting before the place, departed, entrusting the guard and the whole business to the nine archons, with full authority to manage it as they should judge best; at that time it was the nine archons who transacted most of the city's public business.

§9 Those with Cylon, as they were besieged, were in sorry case through want of food and water.

§10 Cylon then, and his brother with him, made their escape; and the rest, hard pressed as they were — and some even dying of the famine — sat down on the altar in the acropolis as suppliants.

§11 Those of the Athenians to whom the guard had been entrusted raised them up — as they saw them dying in the sacred place — on the pledge that they would do them no harm, and leading them away they put them to death; and some of them, as they sat at the altars of the revered gods in the passage, they made away with. From this deed they and the stock descended from them were called men under the curse and guilty before the goddess.

§12 The Athenians, then, drove out these men under the curse; and later Cleomenes the Lacedaemonian drove them out likewise, when the Athenians were in civil strife, driving out the living and digging up the bones of the dead and casting them out; they returned, however, later, and their stock is still in the city.

Chapter 127

§1 This, then, was the curse which the Lacedaemonians bade them drive out, professedly making requital to the gods first; they knew, however, that Pericles son of Xanthippus was implicated in it on his mother's side, and they reckoned that, if he were driven out, their affairs with the Athenians would go forward more easily.

§2 They hoped, however, not so much that this would be inflicted on him as that it would bring a slander against him with the city, to the effect that even through his misfortune the war would in part come about.

§3 For being the most powerful man among his contemporaries and at the head of his city's politics, he was opposed to the Lacedaemonians in everything; and he suffered no yielding, but was driving the Athenians toward the war.

Chapter 128

§1 The Athenians, for their part, bade the Lacedaemonians drive out the curse of Taenarum; for the Lacedaemonians once raised helots who had fled as suppliants to the sanctuary of Poseidon at Taenarum, and leading them away they destroyed them — on account of which they think that the great earthquake in Sparta came upon themselves.

§2 And they bade them also drive out the curse of the goddess of the Brazen House; and the affair was this.

§3 When Pausanias the Lacedaemonian had first been sent for by the Spartans from his command in the Hellespont, and had been tried by them and released as having done no wrong, he was no longer sent out publicly; but on his own he took a trireme of Hermione and, without the Lacedaemonians, arrived in the Hellespont — in word for the war of the Greeks, in deed to transact the business with the King, just as he had made his attempt at the first, being eager for the rule over the Greeks.

§4 From this time forward he first began to lay up a good deed to his credit with the King, and made the beginning of the whole affair.

§5 For he had taken Byzantium at his former coming there, after the retreat from Cyprus — the Medes were holding it, and certain connexions and kinsmen of the King had been captured in it — and then it was that he sent off to the King these whom he had taken, secretly from the other allies, while in word they had run away from him.

§6 He managed these things through Gongylus of Eretria, to whom he had entrusted both Byzantium and the captives. And he also sent a letter, which Gongylus carried to him; and what was written in it was this, as was later brought to light:

§7 “Pausanias, the commander of Sparta, wishing to do you a kindness in these men, sends them to you, taken by the spear; and I form the design, if it also seem good to you, of marrying your daughter, and of bringing Sparta and the rest of Hellas under your hand. And I think I am able to accomplish these things, taking counsel with you. If, then, any of these things pleases you, send a man you can trust to the sea, through whom the rest of our dealings may be carried on.”

Chapter 129

§1 So much the writing declared. And Xerxes was pleased at the letter, and he sent Artabazus son of Pharnaces to the sea with orders to take over the satrapy of Dascylium, dismissing Megabates, who had governed it before, and he sent by him a letter in answer for Pausanias at Byzantium, with instructions to forward it with all speed and to show the seal, and, whatever Pausanias should enjoin on him concerning his own affairs, to do it as well and as faithfully as possible.

§2 He, on arriving, did all the rest as he had been instructed, and had the letter forwarded;

§3 and there was written in reply this: “Thus says King Xerxes to Pausanias. As for the men whom you saved for me across the sea from Byzantium, the good deed will lie recorded for you in our house for ever; and I am pleased with the messages that come from you. And let neither night nor day hold you back, so as to leave undone any of the things you promise me; nor let the spending of gold and silver stop you, nor the size of a host, if one must be present anywhere; but with Artabazus, a good man, whom I have sent to you, carry on in confidence both my affairs and yours, in whatever way will turn out best and fairest for us both.”

Chapter 130

§1 Pausanias, having received this letter — he had before this also been held in great esteem among the Greeks because of his command at Plataeae — was now lifted up far higher, and he could no longer live in the manner that was established; but putting on Median dress he would go out of Byzantium, and as he made his way through Thrace Medes and Egyptians attended him as bodyguard, and he had a Persian table spread before him, and he could not keep his purpose in check — with brief deeds he gave advance sign of what, in the greater reach of his thought, he was going to do thereafter.

§2 And he made himself hard of access, and he used such harsh anger toward all alike that no one was able to come to him; and it was for this reason, not the least, that the alliance passed over to the Athenians.

Chapter 131

§1 The Lacedaemonians had already learned of this behaviour and recalled him the first time for precisely these reasons. When he sailed out a second time in a ship from Hermione without their orders, he was evidently behaving in the same way. The Athenians besieged him in Byzantium and forced him out, but he did not return to Sparta. Instead, he settled at Colonae in the Troad, and reports reached the Lacedaemonians that he was dealing with the barbarians and staying there for no good purpose. They now waited no longer. The ephors sent a herald with a scytale and ordered him to return with the herald: otherwise the Spartans would declare war on him.

§2 Wishing to arouse as little suspicion as possible, and confident that he could use money to dispose of the accusations, he returned to Sparta for the second time. At first the ephors had him imprisoned; they have the power to do this even to a king. Later he secured his release and offered to stand trial if anyone wished to prove the charges against him.

Chapter 132

§1 But neither his enemies nor the city as a whole had any firm evidence on which they could confidently punish a man of royal descent who still held an important office. He was regent for his cousin Pleistarchus, the son of Leonidas, who was king but still a child.

§2 Yet his contempt for the laws and his imitation of the barbarians made people strongly suspect that he would not accept his place as an equal among them. They examined his earlier conduct for any departure from established custom. In particular, on the tripod at Delphi which the Greeks had dedicated as an offering from the first share of the spoils of the Medes, he had once taken it upon himself to have this couplet inscribed: “Pausanias, commander of the Greeks, destroyed the army of the Medes and dedicated this memorial to Phoebus.”

§3 At the time the Lacedaemonians had immediately erased the couplet and inscribed instead the names of all the cities which had joined in defeating the barbarian and dedicating the offering. Even then, this act had been held against Pausanias. Now that he was in his present position, it seemed all the more to have reflected the same ambitions as his current behaviour.

§4 They also learned that he was plotting with the Helots, and this was indeed true. He was promising them freedom and citizenship if they would rise up with him and help him carry his whole plan through.

§5 Even so, the reports of certain Helots did not persuade them to take drastic action against him. They followed their customary practice towards their own citizens: not to decide hastily on an irreversible measure against a Spartan without indisputable evidence. Eventually, the story goes, the man who was to carry his latest letter for the King to Artabazus became an informer. He was from Argilus, had been Pausanias’s lover as a boy and was deeply trusted by him. He grew afraid when he reflected that none of the earlier messengers had ever returned. He made a copy of the seal, so that Pausanias would not discover what he had done if his suspicions proved wrong or Pausanias asked to alter the letter. Then he opened it and found, as he had suspected, an additional instruction ordering his own death too.

Chapter 133

§1 When the man showed them the letter, the ephors were more convinced, but they still wanted to hear Pausanias himself say something. By prior arrangement, the man went as a suppliant to Taenarum, built a hut divided into two compartments by a partition, and hid some of the ephors inside. When Pausanias came and asked why he had sought sanctuary, they heard everything clearly. The man complained about what the letter said about him and set out the other points one by one: he had never betrayed Pausanias in his services to the king, yet his special reward was to die just like the other messengers. Pausanias acknowledged all this. He urged him not to be angry about what had happened, gave him assurances that he could leave the sanctuary safely, and asked him to set out as quickly as possible and not obstruct their business.

Chapter 134

§1 After hearing everything clearly, the ephors left. Now certain of the facts, they set about arresting him in the city. It is said that, when he was about to be arrested in the street, he understood why one of the ephors was approaching from the expression on his face. Another secretly nodded to him, giving him a friendly warning. Pausanias ran to the sanctuary of the goddess of the Brazen House, whose precinct was nearby, and reached it before he could be caught. To avoid having to stay in the open, he went into a small building belonging to the sanctuary and remained there.

§2 His pursuers were too late to catch him then. Afterwards they removed the building’s roof, made sure he was inside and walled up the doors, trapping him within. They kept watch outside and starved him out.

§3 When they realized that he was about to breathe his last there in the building, they carried him out of the sanctuary while he was still alive. He died as soon as he had been brought outside.

§4 They were going to throw his body into the Caeadas, where they throw criminals, but then decided to bury him somewhere nearby. Later, the god at Delphi ordered the Lacedaemonians to move his grave to the place where he had died. He now lies in the forecourt of the sanctuary, as the inscribed slabs show. The god also declared that their act had brought religious pollution upon them and ordered them to repay the goddess of the Brazen House with two bodies for one. They made two bronze statues and dedicated them in place of Pausanias.

Chapter 135

§1 The Athenians in turn demanded that the Lacedaemonians remove this pollution, pointing out that the god himself had condemned it.

§2 The Lacedaemonians sent envoys to Athens to accuse Themistocles too of involvement in Pausanias’s dealings with the Medes. They based the charge on evidence from the inquiry into Pausanias and demanded that Themistocles receive the same punishment.

§3 The Athenians agreed. Themistocles had been ostracized and was living in Argos, though he also visited other parts of the Peloponnese. They sent men with orders to arrest him wherever they found him, together with the Lacedaemonians, who were ready to join the pursuit.

Chapter 136

§1 Themistocles learned of this in advance and fled from the Peloponnese to Corcyra, whose people he had benefited. The Corcyraeans, however, said they were afraid to shelter him and incur the hostility of the Lacedaemonians and Athenians. They took him across to the opposite mainland.

§2 The men sent to arrest him pursued him, following reports of his movements. Finding himself with nowhere to turn, he was forced to seek lodging with Admetus, king of the Molossians, who was no friend of his.

§3 Admetus happened to be away. Themistocles appealed to his wife as a suppliant, and she told him to take their child in his arms and sit down at the hearth.

§4 When Admetus returned soon afterwards, Themistocles told him who he was. He urged him not to take revenge on a fugitive for any opposition he might once have offered when Admetus sought help from the Athenians. In his present position, even someone much weaker than Admetus could harm him, whereas honourable revenge was taken between equals on equal terms. Besides, his own opposition had concerned some advantage Admetus wanted, not his personal safety. If Admetus handed him over, however, he would deprive him of his life. Themistocles also explained who was pursuing him and why. [Note: “Weaker” is understood here as “someone much weaker than Admetus”, following the wording of the Greek base text. A variant reading describes Themistocles himself as weaker.]

Chapter 137

§1 After hearing him, Admetus raised him to his feet together with his own son, whom Themistocles had been holding as he sat. This was the most compelling form of supplication. When the Lacedaemonians and Athenians arrived shortly afterwards, Admetus refused to surrender him despite all their arguments. Themistocles wished to go to the King, so Admetus sent him overland to the other sea, to Pydna in Alexander’s territory.

§2 There he found a merchant ship about to sail for Ionia and went aboard. A storm drove it towards the Athenian force besieging Naxos. No one on the ship knew who he was. In fear, he revealed his identity to the shipmaster and explained why he was fleeing. If the man did not save him, he said, he would declare that the shipmaster had accepted money to carry him. The safe course was to let no one leave the ship until they could sail on. If the shipmaster complied, he would reward him properly. The man did as he asked, kept offshore beyond the Athenian camp for a day and a night, and later reached Ephesus.

§3 Themistocles rewarded the shipmaster with money when funds later reached him from his friends in Athens and from what he had put aside at Argos. He travelled inland with one of the Persians from the coast and sent ahead a letter to King Artaxerxes, son of Xerxes, who had recently come to the throne.

§4 The letter read: “I, Themistocles, have come to you. Of all the Greeks, I did your house the greatest harm while I was forced to defend myself against your father’s invasion. But I did him far greater good when he was retreating, at a time when I was safe and he was in danger. I am owed a favour in return.” Here he mentioned the advance warning he had sent from Salamis about the retreat, and the fact that the bridges had not been destroyed, for which he falsely claimed credit. “Now I have come to you, able to render you great services, pursued by the Greeks because of my friendship with you. I wish to wait a year and then explain to you in person why I have come.”

Chapter 138

§1 The King, it is said, admired his purpose and told him to do as he proposed. During the waiting period, Themistocles learned as much as he could of the Persian language and the customs of the country.

§2 When he came to the King after that year, he gained greater standing at court than any Greek before him. This was due partly to his existing reputation and partly to the hope he offered the King of enslaving the Greeks. Above all, it was because his abilities became clear when put to the test.

§3 Themistocles demonstrated the power of natural talent with exceptional clarity, and in this respect deserved admiration beyond anyone else. His intelligence was his own: he had neither acquired it through earlier study nor developed it through later learning. With very little deliberation, he was the best judge of immediate problems; he was also the best at predicting what would happen in the most distant future. He could explain the matters he was engaged in, and even where he lacked experience he could still form a sound judgement. Better than anyone, he foresaw the good or bad in a situation while it was still hidden. In a word, his natural ability and the little preparation he needed made him the best at deciding on the spot what had to be done.

§4 He fell ill and died. Some, however, say that he deliberately took poison because he thought he could not fulfil his promises to the King.

§5 His monument stands in the marketplace of Magnesia in Asia. He governed that region: the King had given him Magnesia, which yielded fifty talents a year, to provide his bread; Lampsacus, then regarded as the richest place in wine, to provide his wine; and Myus to provide his other food.

§6 His relatives say that, at his own request, his bones were brought home and buried in Attica without the Athenians’ knowledge. Burial there was forbidden because he was an exile on a charge of treason. Such was the end of Pausanias the Lacedaemonian and Themistocles the Athenian, the most illustrious Greeks of their time.

Chapter 139

§1 These were the demands the Lacedaemonians made on their first embassy, and the demands they received in return, concerning the expulsion of those under a religious curse. On later visits to Athens they demanded withdrawal from Potidaea and autonomy for Aegina. Above all, they stated most emphatically and clearly that there would be no war if the Athenians repealed the Megarian decree. This decree barred the Megarians from using the harbours of the Athenian empire and the market in Attica.

§2 The Athenians refused both to meet the other demands and to repeal the decree. They accused the Megarians of cultivating the sacred land and the land with no fixed boundary, and of sheltering runaway slaves.

§3 Finally, the last envoys from Lacedaemon arrived: Rhamphias, Melesippus and Agesander. They repeated none of the earlier demands, but said only this: “The Lacedaemonians want peace, and there could be peace if you allowed the Greeks their autonomy.” The Athenians convened an assembly and considered proposals among themselves. They decided to discuss all the issues together and give a single reply.

§4 Many speakers came forward on both sides. Some argued for war; others said the decree should be repealed rather than allowed to obstruct peace. Then Pericles, son of Xanthippus, came forward. He was the leading Athenian of his time, with the greatest ability in both speech and action. He gave the following advice.

Chapter 140

§1 Athenians, I hold to the same view as always: we must not give in to the Peloponnesians. I know that people’s enthusiasm when they are persuaded to go to war differs from their spirit when they are actually fighting it, and that their opinions change with their fortunes. Even so, I see that I must give you much the same advice now. Those of you who agree with me must stand by our common decisions even if we suffer a setback; otherwise, you must not claim a share of the credit for sound judgement if we succeed. Events can unfold as unpredictably as human plans. That is why we habitually blame chance for anything that happens contrary to our expectations.

§2 The Lacedaemonians have clearly been plotting against us before, and they are doing so just as clearly now. Our agreement provides that we are to submit our disputes to arbitration and accept it from each other, and that both sides are to keep what we hold. Yet they have never asked for arbitration themselves and refuse it when we offer it. They want to settle their grievances through war rather than discussion. They now come to issue orders, no longer to bring accusations.

§3 They order us to withdraw from Potidaea, grant Aegina its autonomy and repeal the Megarian decree. These latest envoys also demand that we allow the Greeks their autonomy.

§4 None of you should think that, if we refuse to repeal the Megarian decree, we will be fighting over a trifle. They put this demand above all the others, claiming that there would be no war if we repealed it. Do not leave any grounds for accusing yourselves later of having gone to war over a small matter.

§5 This small matter is the full test and confirmation of your resolve. If you give in to them here, they will immediately order you to do something more serious, convinced that fear made you obey the first demand. If you firmly refuse, you will make it clear that they should deal with you as equals.

Chapter 141

§1 Decide here and now: either obey before you suffer any harm, or, if we are to fight, as I think we should, resolve not to give way over either a great matter or a small one, and not to live in fear for our possessions. Any demand imposed by an equal on a neighbour before arbitration, however large or small, amounts to the same enslavement.

§2 Listen to my account of the military situation and the resources of each side, point by point, and you will see that we will not be the weaker side.

§3 The Peloponnesians work their own farms and have no money, either privately or in their public treasuries. They have no experience of long wars or overseas campaigns: poverty limits them to brief attacks on one another.

§4 People in their position cannot regularly man fleets or send armies into the field. They would be away from their own farms while paying their own expenses, and they would also be cut off from the sea.

§5 It is accumulated wealth that sustains wars, more than forced contributions. Farmers who work their own land are readier to risk their lives in war than their money. They trust that they may survive the dangers, but cannot be sure that their money will not run out first, especially if the war lasts longer than they expect, as it probably will.

§6 In a single battle, the Peloponnesians and their allies can stand against all the Greeks together. But they cannot sustain a war against an opponent whose resources differ from their own. They have no single council to make prompt decisions, and with each people having an equal vote but belonging to a different stock, everyone pursues their own interests. Usually, nothing gets accomplished as a result.

§7 Some want above all to take revenge on someone; others want above all to preserve their own property. They take a long time to assemble, then spend only a small part of their time on common concerns and most of it on their own affairs. Each thinks that their own neglect will do no harm, and that someone else will take care of their interests. Because everyone privately reasons in the same way, the common interest is collectively ruined without their noticing.

Chapter 142

§1 Their greatest obstacle will be a shortage of money. Raising it slowly will cause delays, and opportunities in war do not wait.

§2 Nor do we need to fear either their building a fortified position against us or their fleet.

§3 Even in peacetime it would be difficult to build a city to rival ours, let alone in enemy territory, with us facing them from fortifications just as strong.

§4 If they build a fort, they may damage part of our land through raids and by encouraging desertions. But it will not stop us sailing to their territory to build a fort of our own, or using our ships, our main strength, to retaliate.

§5 Our experience at sea has taught us more about fighting on land than their experience on land has taught them about fighting at sea.

§6 They will not easily become skilled at naval warfare.

§7 Even you, who have practised it ever since the Median Wars, have not yet perfected it. How could farmers with no seafaring background achieve anything worthwhile? They will not even be allowed to practise, because we will constantly keep large numbers of ships stationed against them.

§8 If only a few ships were stationed against them, they might risk an engagement, their numbers giving them confidence despite their inexperience. But blockaded by many ships, they will stay inactive. Without practice they will become less skilled, and therefore less willing to venture out.

§9 Naval warfare is a skill like any other. It cannot be practised as a sideline whenever the opportunity arises; indeed, it leaves no room for any other sideline at all.

Chapter 143

§1 Suppose they draw on the funds at Olympia or Delphi and try to lure away the foreigners among our sailors with higher pay. That would be serious if we could not match them by manning the ships ourselves, together with our resident foreigners. But we can. Above all, among our citizens we have more and better helmsmen and other naval personnel than all the rest of Greece combined.

§2 Given the risks, no foreign sailor would accept exile from his own country to fight on their side, with worse prospects of success, for the sake of a few days’ higher pay.

§3 That, as I see it, is broadly the position of the Peloponnesians. Our position is free of the faults I have pointed out in theirs, and we have other great advantages which they cannot match.

§4 If they invade our land with their army, we will sail against theirs. Devastating a part of the Peloponnese and devastating all of Attica will not have equivalent effects: they cannot obtain other land in exchange without fighting, whereas we have plenty of land both on islands and on the mainland.

§5 Command of the sea is a great power. Think about it: if we were islanders, who would be harder to conquer? Even now, we should act as nearly as possible on that principle. We must give up our land and houses and guard the sea and the city. Anger over our property must not draw us into battle with the Peloponnesians, who greatly outnumber us. If we win, we will have to fight an equally large force again. If we lose, we will also lose our hold on the allies, on which our strength depends: they will not remain quiet if we are no longer strong enough to send armies against them. We must mourn lives, not houses and land. People acquire these possessions; the possessions do not acquire people. If I thought I could persuade you, I would tell you to go out and destroy your property yourselves, to show the Peloponnesians that you will not submit for its sake.

Chapter 144

§1 I have many other reasons to hope that we will prevail, provided you do not try to expand our empire while we are at war or take on additional dangers of your own choosing. I fear our own mistakes more than our enemies’ plans.

§2 These matters can be explained in another speech as events require. For now, let us send the envoys away with this answer: we will allow the Megarians to use our market and harbours if the Lacedaemonians also stop expelling us and our allies as foreigners, since neither practice is forbidden by the truce. We will leave the cities autonomous if they were autonomous under us when we made the truce, and when the Lacedaemonians in turn allow their own cities to govern themselves according to each city's wishes, rather than to suit Sparta. We are willing to submit to arbitration under the agreements. We will not start a war, but we will defend ourselves against those who do. This answer is both just and worthy of our city.

§3 You must understand that war is necessary. If we accept it more willingly, our enemies will press us less hard. You must also understand that the greatest dangers bring the greatest honours, both to a city and to an individual.

§4 Our fathers stood against the Medes with fewer resources than we have, and even abandoned what they possessed. Through judgement more than luck, and courage more than strength, they drove back the barbarian and brought our resources to their present level. We must live up to their example, resist our enemies by every means and try to pass on what we have to the next generation undiminished.

Chapter 145

§1 This was what Pericles said. The Athenians judged his advice the best for them and voted as he proposed. Their reply to the Lacedaemonians followed his recommendations, both on the individual points and in general: they would do nothing under orders, but were ready to settle the grievances by arbitration under the agreements, on equal terms. The envoys went home, and no further embassies followed.
