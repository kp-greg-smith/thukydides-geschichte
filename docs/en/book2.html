Thucydides: The Peloponnesian War

Translation principles

Translate directly from Ancient Greek, preserving the meaning in clear prose. Other translations may help resolve questions of interpretation, but do not serve as the source and must not be copied.

Language. Preserve the full meaning of the Greek in English that sounds as natural and modern as possible, as a present-day author would express the same content. Greek sentence structure is not a model for English prose. Judge readability in context, without mechanical rules for sentence length or form.

  1. A stable equivalent for each meaning. Do not force one word for every occurrence. αἰτία may mean accusation, cause, blame, or responsibility; a causal rendering is not excluded where the Greek sense requires it.
  2. Nothing added, nothing exaggerated. No explanations, imagery, claims, or emphasis absent from the Greek.
  3. Nothing softened. The force of harsh statements and of political vocabulary is preserved; δουλεία remains slavery in that sense.
  4. Notes for genuine uncertainty. Brief notes for textual uncertainty, genuine interpretive questions, or a significant change in a key term’s sense. Notes are not limited to damaged or corrupt text.
  5. Ambiguity preserved. Where the Greek allows more than one reading, that openness is kept where possible; unavoidable choices are explained in a brief note.
  6. Extra care with speeches and indirect speech. Speaker, addressee, argument, reported viewpoint, conditions, negation, modality, and temporal relations are preserved. Reported claims do not become the narrator’s assertions.

Chapter 1

§1 From this point the war of the Athenians and the Peloponnesians and of the allies of each begins — the war in which they no longer came together with one another unheralded, but, once drawn up against each other, fought continuously; and what follows is written down in order, as each event occurred, by summers and winters.

Chapter 2

§1 For the thirty years' truce, which was made after the capture of Euboea, held good for fourteen years; in the fifteenth year, when Chrysis at Argos had been priestess for fifty years less two, Aenesius was ephor at Sparta, and Pythodorus was archon of the Athenians with two months of his term still to run, in the sixth month after the battle of Potidaea, and just as spring was beginning, Thebans, a little more than three hundred in number, entered Plataea — a city of Boeotia allied to the Athenians — at about the hour of first sleep, under arms (their leaders were the boeotarchs Pythangelus son of Phyleides and Diemporus son of Onetorides).

§2 The Thebans had been let in, and the gates opened for them, by men of Plataea — Naucleides and those with him — who wished, for the sake of their own private power, to destroy such of their fellow-citizens as were opposed to them and to make the city over to the Thebans.

§3 This was done through Eurymachus son of Leontiades, a man of the greatest power among the Thebans. The Thebans, foreseeing that the war would come, wished to seize Plataea beforehand — a city that had always been a difference between them — while it was still peacetime and the war was not yet manifest; and for that reason they slipped in the more easily, no watch having been posted ahead of them.

§4 Standing their arms together in the market-place, they did not obey those who had let them in, to set their hands to the work at once and go against the houses of their enemies; instead they resolved to make use of fitting proclamations, and to bring the city rather to agreement and friendship (and the herald made proclamation that if anyone wished to serve as an ally according to the ancestral usage of all the Boeotians, he should lay down his arms beside them [Note: beside the Thebans, signifying that he joined their side.]), believing that in this way the city would readily come over to them.

Chapter 3

§1 The Plataeans, when they became aware that the Thebans were inside and that the city had been seized on a sudden, were dismayed, and supposing that far more had entered (for in the night they could not see), came to terms, and, having accepted the offers, remained quiet — especially as the Thebans were taking no hostile action against anyone.

§2 While this was being arranged, they somehow perceived that the Thebans were not many, and judged that by attacking they would easily get the better of them; for the mass of the Plataeans was unwilling to revolt from the Athenians.

§3 They resolved, accordingly, to make the attempt; and they came together, breaking through the party walls that divided house from house, so that in going to and fro they might not be seen in the streets; they also set wagons, without their draught-animals, in the streets to serve instead of a wall, and made every other preparation, as each thing seemed likely to prove useful for the circumstances of the moment.

§4 And when everything was as ready as their means allowed, they waited for the moment — it was still night, at the very point before daybreak — and came out of their houses upon the Thebans, so that they might not assault men made bolder by the light and find themselves on even terms with them, but so that in the dark, being the more alarmed, the Thebans might prove inferior through the Plataeans' own experience of the city; and they attacked at once, and came swiftly to close quarters.

Chapter 4

§1 The Thebans, as soon as they knew that they had been cheated, drew themselves up close together, and beat off the attacks wherever these were delivered.

§2 Twice or three times they repulsed them; then, with a great din arising — the Plataeans charging them, and the women and slaves crying out and wailing together from the houses and pelting them with stones and tiles, while at the same time a heavy rain in the night came on — they took fright, and turned and fled through the city. Most of them were unacquainted, amid the darkness and the mud, with the lanes through which they needed to find safety (for what was happening occurred at the end of the month), and they had as pursuers men who knew the ways, skilled to prevent escape, so that the greater number perished.

§3 And one of the Plataeans closed the gates through which the Thebans had entered — these alone had been opened — thrusting into the bar, in place of the bolt, the butt of a javelin, so that there was no longer any exit even there.

§4 Pursued through the city, some of them climbed upon the wall and flung themselves down outside, and the greater number of these perished; others, at gates where none stood guard, secretly received an axe from a woman who handed it them, and cutting through the bar made their way out — not many, for the matter was quickly noticed — and others again, scattered this way and that about the city, were killed.

§5 The largest body of them, the group that was most closely banded together, burst into a great building which formed part of the wall — its doors happened to stand open — supposing that the doors of the building were gates and that there was a passage straight out.

§6 The Plataeans, seeing them trapped, deliberated whether to burn them as they were, by setting the building alight, or to adopt some other measure.

§7 In the end these men, and all the other Thebans who survived, as they wandered through the city, came to an agreement with the Plataeans to surrender themselves and their arms, the Plataeans to deal with them as they pleased.

§8 The men at Plataea, then, fared thus.

Chapter 5

§1 As for the rest of the Thebans — those whose task it was to come up with the whole levy while the night still remained, in case the enterprise of the men who had entered should miscarry — the news of what had occurred was announced to them on the march, and they at once set out to the help of their fellows.

§2 Plataea is seventy stades distant from Thebes, and the water that came down in the night made their coming slower; for the river Asopus had risen in flood, and it was not easily crossed.

§3 Marching through the rain and crossing the river only with difficulty, they arrived too late: of their men some had already been destroyed, and those still alive were held captive.

§4 When the Thebans learned what had happened, they plotted against the Plataeans who were outside the city; for there were men too in the fields, and property, such as there is when unexpected harm befalls in a time of peace; and they wished, if they could catch any, to have them in hand to set against the men inside, in case some of their own should chance to have been taken alive. Such was the design of the Thebans;

§5 the Plataeans, while the Thebans were still deliberating, suspected that some such thing was likely, and, in fear for those outside, sent out a herald to the Thebans, telling them that what had been done was unholy — attempting, while a truce bound both parties, to seize their city — and that they must do no wrong to those outside; otherwise, they declared, they would themselves kill the Thebans whom they held alive; but if the Thebans retired once more from the territory, they would restore the men to them.

§6 This is the Theban account, and they say that the Plataeans swore an oath to it; the Plataeans, for their part, do not admit that they promised to give the men back immediately — only, they say, after words had first passed, and if some agreement should be reached — and they deny that they swore.

§7 The Thebans accordingly withdrew from the territory, having done no wrong; and the Plataeans, having with all speed brought in from the countryside everything that was outside, immediately put the men to death. The prisoners numbered one hundred and eighty in all, one of whom was Eurymachus, the man for whose sake the traitors had acted.

Chapter 6

§1 Having done this, the Plataeans sent a messenger to Athens and gave back the dead to the Thebans under truce, and they put the affairs of the city in such order as seemed best to them for the moment.

§2 To the Athenians the events at Plataea were reported immediately; and they at once seized such Boeotians as were in Attica, and sent a herald to Plataea, with instructions to tell the Plataeans to do nothing rash about the Thebans whom they held, before the Athenians themselves should have deliberated on the matter —

§3 — for it had not been reported to them that the men were dead. At the very moment of the Thebans' entry the first messenger was setting out from Plataea, the second just when the Thebans had been defeated and taken; and of what came after the Athenians knew nothing. In this ignorance they kept sending their instructions; and the herald, on his arrival, found the men already destroyed.

§4 After this the Athenians marched to Plataea, brought in grain, left a garrison behind, and carried out of the city the least serviceable of the people, together with the women and the children.

Chapter 7

§1 After the affair at Plataea, and the dissolution of the truce which had been brought about in so striking a manner, the Athenians were making their preparations for the war, and so were the Lacedaemonians and their allies; and both sides were minded to send embassies to the King and elsewhere among the barbarians, wherever either hoped to attach some help to itself, and were bringing into alliance such cities as lay outside the range of their own power.

§2 The Lacedaemonians, then, ordered those in Italy and Sicily who had chosen their side to build ships besides those they already had, in proportion to the size of the cities, so that the total number would come to five hundred, and to get a specified sum of money ready, meanwhile keeping quiet in everything else, and receiving the Athenians when they came with but a single ship, until these preparations were complete.

§3 The Athenians held a close review of the alliance they already had, and sent embassies to the regions lying more particularly round the Peloponnese — to Corcyra, Cephallenia, the Acarnanians and Zacynthus — seeing that, if these stood fast as their friends, they would subdue the Peloponnese waging war upon it from all round.

Chapter 8

§1 The designs of the two sides were nothing small, and they pressed eagerly into the war not unreasonably; for at the beginning all men take hold the more keenly; and at that time the young — plentiful in the Peloponnese, plentiful too at Athens — through inexperience laid hands on the war not unwillingly; and all the rest of Hellas hung in suspense as the leading cities closed with one another.

§2 Many prophetic sayings were repeated, and the oracle-mongers sang many things, among those on the point of making war and among the other cities alike.

§3 Not long before these things Delos, too, was shaken by an earthquake — it had never before been shaken, in all the time the Greeks have memory; and it was said, and believed, to be a sign of what was going to come to pass; and whatever else of the kind chanced to happen — everything of it was searched into.

§4 The goodwill of men inclined by far the more towards the Lacedaemonians, especially as they had proclaimed beforehand that they were liberating Hellas; and all were eagerly set on joining in lending them aid, private men and cities, with whatever they could, in word and deed; and it seemed to each that any undertaking in which he himself did not take part would be held up.

§5 So, then, did the majority hold the Athenians in anger — the one party wishing to be released from their rule, the other fearing to be put under it.

Chapter 9

§1 With such preparations and such designs, then, the two sides entered upon the war; the allies each had were the following.

§2 The allies of the Lacedaemonians were these: all the Peloponnesians within the Isthmus, except the Argives and the Achaeans (these two peoples were in friendship with both sides; of the Achaeans, at the first, only the men of Pellene shared in the war, though later all did); and outside the Peloponnese the Megarians, the Boeotians, the Locrians, the Phocians, the Ambraciots, the Leucadians, the Anactorians.

§3 Of these the Corinthians, the Megarians, the Sicyonians, the men of Pellene, the Eleians, the Ambraciots and the Leucadians furnished the fleet; the Boeotians, the Phocians and the Locrians furnished cavalry; the remaining cities furnished infantry. Such was the alliance of the Lacedaemonians.

§4 The allies of the Athenians were the Chians, the Lesbians, the Plataeans, the Messenians in Naupactus, most of the Acarnanians, the Corcyraeans and the Zacynthians, together with the other subject cities among the following peoples: Caria that lies along the sea, the Dorians adjoining the Carians, Ionia, the Hellespont, the places in the region of Thrace, and the islands, so many of them as lie between the Peloponnese and Crete towards the rising sun — namely, all the Cyclades except Melos and Thera.

§5 Of these the Chians, the Lesbians and the Corcyraeans furnished the fleet; the rest furnished infantry and money.

§6 Such was the alliance of each side and their preparation for the war.

Chapter 10

§1 The Lacedaemonians, immediately after the affair at Plataea, sent notice round through the Peloponnese and to their allies outside it, directing the several cities to prepare an army, and to have with them such supplies as are customary for an expedition abroad, intending to march into Attica.

§2 When each had what was needed ready, the two-thirds from each city came together at the appointed time at the Isthmus.

§3 And when the whole army was assembled, Archidamus, the king of the Lacedaemonians, the man who was in command of this expedition, called together the commanders from all the cities, and also the men most in office and of the highest standing, and spoke to this effect.

Chapter 11

§1 Men of the Peloponnese and allies, our fathers made many campaigns both within the Peloponnese itself and outside it, and among ourselves the elder men are not without experience of wars; yet never before have we set out with a greater armament than now — nay, we are marching upon the most powerful of cities, and that with ourselves taking the field most numerous and best.

§2 It is right, then, that we should show ourselves neither worse than our fathers nor lacking in regard to the repute that is ours. All Hellas is stirred by this onset of ours and fixes its attention upon it, regarding us with good will — through hatred of the Athenians — that we may accomplish what we intend.

§3 Yet it is not right that, if we seem to some to be advancing by weight of numbers, and it seems that there is great security in that the enemy would not be likely to come to battle with us, on those grounds we should proceed with something more of carelessness in our preparations; rather must every man of each several city, commander and soldier alike, expect always, for his own part, that he will come into some danger.

§4 For the issues of war are dark, and attacks mostly arise from small beginnings and in anger; and often a smaller number, because it is afraid, has defended itself all the better against a greater which through contempt has come unprepared.

§5 In the enemy’s land one must always campaign with mind confident, but in deed be prepared as one who fears; for so would men be boldest to press upon the enemy, and most secure in being attacked.

§6 As for us, we are not marching against a city so powerless to defend itself; it is one prepared in every respect in the best manner — so that one must expect in full measure that they will come to battle, unless they have even now set out while we are not yet there, but will do so when they see us in their land, ravaging and destroying their possessions.

§7 For all men anger comes over them when, before their very eyes and on the instant, they see some suffering they are not used to; and those who make least use of reasoning carry most into action in their wrath.

§8 And the Athenians above all others may well be expected to do this — men who claim to rule over others, and who in their invasions are more for ravaging other men’s land than for beholding their own.

§9 Since, then, we are marching against a city of such greatness, and shall carry home the highest repute to our forefathers and ourselves — whichever way the outcome turns — follow wherever a man shall lead, maintaining everywhere good order and watchfulness, and receiving promptly the orders that are given; for this is the fairest and the safest course, that men who are many should be seen to keep a single discipline.

Chapter 12

§1 Having said these words and dissolved the council, Archidamus sent first to Athens Melesippus son of Diacritus, a Spartan, in case the Athenians, seeing them already on their way, might be the more inclined to yield.

§2 The Athenians did not receive him into the city, nor before the assembled public; for a proposal of Pericles had already been carried, that no herald and no embassy should be received from the Lacedaemonians once they had taken the field with an army. They sent him off, therefore, without hearing him, and bade him be outside the frontiers that very day; and for what remained, when they had retired to their own country, they might, if they wished, send an embassy. And they sent escorts along with Melesippus as well, so that he should have dealings with no one.

§3 When he had come to the borders and was about to part, he went his way, having said only this much: “This day will begin great evils for the Greeks.”

§4 When he had come back to the camp, and Archidamus had learned that the Athenians would now yield nothing at all, at last he took up his army and advanced into their land.

§5 The Boeotians furnished their own contingent, and their cavalry, to campaign together with the Peloponnesians; with the rest that were left they marched against Plataea and were ravaging the land.

Chapter 13

§1 While the Peloponnesians were still gathering at the Isthmus and still on their way, before they broke into Attica, Pericles son of Xanthippus, then general of the Athenians — himself one of the ten — as soon as he learned that the invasion was to come, suspected that Archidamus, who chanced to be his guest-friend, might perhaps pass his lands by and not ravage them — whether because he wished in a private kindness to gratify him, or even because the Lacedaemonians had so commanded, with a view to his own discrediting, even as they had before demanded that the pollutions be driven out on his account — and he proclaimed beforehand to the Athenians in the assembly that Archidamus was indeed his guest-friend, but that this should not be to the harm of the city; and that as for his own lands and houses, if indeed the enemy did not ravage them as they would those of the rest, he made them over to be public property, and that no suspicion should on this account be had of him.

§2 In advising on the matters before them he advised the same things as before: to make their preparations for the war, and to bring in their property from the fields; not to march out to battle, but to enter within the city and guard it; to get the fleet ready, that being wherein they were strong; and to keep the allies well in hand — saying that their power came from these — the monies of the tribute brought in from the allies — and that in war the greater part is decided by judgement and by a surplus of money.

§3 And he bade them be of good courage, there coming in to the city from the allies each year, as a rule, a tribute of six hundred talents over and above the other income, and there lying at that time in the acropolis six thousand talents of coined silver (for at its highest it had come to ten thousand talents, less three hundred, out of which had been spent the propylaea of the acropolis and the other buildings, and the expenses upon Potidaea).

§4 Besides, there was uncoined gold and silver in the votive offerings, both private and public, and all the sacred vessels used in the processions and the games, and the Median spoils, and anything else of that kind, amounting to not less than five hundred talents.

§5 To these he added also the not inconsiderable monies from the other sanctuaries besides, which they might use, and — if they should be quite shut out from all else — the gold that lay about the very goddess herself; and he declared that the image carried forty talents’ weight of refined gold, and that it was all removable; and that those who had used it for their preservation were bound to set it back again in no less measure.

§6 On money, then, he heartened them after this fashion; and the hoplites he declared to number thirteen thousand, besides those in the fortresses and the sixteen thousand who kept their station along the ramparts.

§7 For so many kept watch at first, whenever the enemy should invade — drawn both from the eldest and from the youngest, and from such of the metics as bore the arms of hoplites. The Phaleric wall, as far as the circuit of the city, was of thirty-five stades; and of that circuit itself the guarded part was of forty-three (there is of it a part that was even left unguarded, the stretch between the long wall and the Phaleric); the long walls towards the Piraeus were of forty stades, of which the outer face was watched; and of the Piraeus together with Munychia the whole circuit was of sixty stades, of which half was under guard.

§8 The cavalry, including the mounted archers, he declared to be twelve hundred in number; the archers sixteen hundred; and the seaworthy triremes three hundred.

§9 For these were the resources the Athenians had, and each item of them no less, when the first invasion of the Peloponnesians was about to come and they were entering upon the war. And Pericles spoke also the other things he was accustomed to speak, by way of demonstrating that they would prevail in the war.

Chapter 14

§1 And the Athenians, when they heard it, were persuaded, and began to bring in from the fields the children and the women and the rest of the household furnishings with which they were used to live at home; and they even took down the woodwork of the houses themselves. The sheep and the beasts of burden they sent over to Euboea and to the islands that lay near.

§2 And this removal was a painful thing to them, because the mass of them had always been accustomed to have their daily life in the fields.

Chapter 15

§1 This had befallen the Athenians more than others from the very earliest times. For under Cecrops and the first kings, down to Theseus, Attica was always settled town by town, each town having its own prytaneum and its own magistrates; and, save whenever they were afraid of some danger, they did not come together to deliberate with the king, but governed themselves and took counsel each on its own account; and some of them even went to war at times, as the Eleusinians once, with Eumolpus, fought against Erechtheus.

§2 But when Theseus had come to the kingship — being a man of understanding joined with power — he ordered the country in the other ways as well; and, having abolished the council-houses and the magistracies of the other towns, and appointed one council-house and one prytaneum, he gathered them all together into the city that now is, and compelled them to use this one city as their single city, though each still possessed what he had before, as in former days; she, all men now contributing to her, being grown great, was handed on by Theseus to those who came after; and from that time the Athenians celebrate even to this day for the goddess the Synoecia, a festival at the public charge.

§3 Before that time the acropolis of today was the city, together with the ground beneath it, which is turned for the most part towards the south.

§4 A proof of this: the sanctuaries in the acropolis itself are of other gods as well, and those outside are founded rather in this part of the city — that of Olympian Zeus, the Pythium, that of Earth, and that of Dionysus in the Marshes, at which the more ancient Dionysia are performed on the twelfth day, in the month Anthesterion, just as the Ionians who are descended from the Athenians still observe them to this day; and other ancient sanctuaries besides are founded in this quarter. [Note: the source edition marks the transmitted Greek of this sentence as corrupt at this point; it is rendered here according to its evident sense.]

§5 And there is the spring which now is called the Enneacrunos — the tyrants having given it its present form — but in ancient times was named Callirrhoë, when its springs ran open: the men of those days, since it lay near, used it for the things of most account; and even now, from ancient custom, it is still the practice to use the water before marriage and in the other sacred things.

§6 And from the ancient settlement that was made there, the acropolis too is called the city by the Athenians even to this day.

Chapter 16

§1 The Athenians had long pursued a manner of dwelling that was, for the most part throughout the land, autonomous; and when they had been gathered into the one city, the greater number of them nevertheless — the old families and those born later alike, down to this war, having been born and having lived by their habit in the fields — did not easily make their removals taking their whole households with them, and this above all because they had only just taken up again the furnishings of their homes after the Median war.

§2 They were grieved, and bore it grievously — leaving behind their houses and the sanctuaries which, under the ancient ordering of the state, had always been theirs by inheritance — and now about to change their manner of life, with each man leaving behind nothing other than his own city.

Chapter 17

§1 When they had come into the town, a few there were who had lodgings, or refuge with some friends or kinsmen; the mass of them settled in the empty quarters of the city, and in the temples and the shrines of the heroes — in all of them, that is, except the acropolis and the Eleusinium, and any other place that was firmly shut. The quarter called the Pelargic, under the acropolis — which was accursed ground, not to be dwelt in, and which moreover a closing utterance of the Pythian oracle forbade in words such as these: “the Pelargic unused is better” — nevertheless, under the pressure of the immediate need, came to be inhabited.

§2 And to me it seems that the oracle fell out the very opposite of what they were expecting; for the misfortunes that befell the city did not come about because of the unlawful habitation; the necessity of the habitation came from the war, which the oracle foresaw, though it did not name it, knowing that the place would never at any time be inhabited for any good.

§3 Many of them besides made shift for quarters in the towers of the walls, each as he was able; for the city did not have room for them all when they were gathered together; but later they parceled out among themselves and settled also the long walls and most of the Piraeus.

§4 At the same time they set their hands to the business of the war besides — gathering in their allies and fitting out a hundred ships to sail against the Peloponnese.

§5 The Athenians, meanwhile, were engaged upon their preparations.

Chapter 18

§1 And the army of the Peloponnesians, advancing, came first in Attica to Oenoe — the very place at which they were minded to break in. When they had encamped, they set about preparing assaults upon the wall, intending to deliver them both by engines and in other ways as well.

§2 For Oenoe, lying in the borderland of Attica and Boeotia, was fortified with a wall, and the Athenians used the place as a fort whenever war came upon them. They therefore made their assaults ready, and otherwise also whiled away time about it.

§3 On this account Archidamus drew blame that was not slight — being thought, at the very opening of the war, slack, and a man favourably disposed towards the Athenians, since he did not counsel prosecuting the war with zeal; and while the army was still assembling, both the stay that was made at the Isthmus and the slowness of the rest of the march told against him — most of all the halt at Oenoe.

§4 For during this time the Athenians were bringing in their property; and the Peloponnesians, it was thought, would with one rapid onset have seized everything that was still outside, had it not been for that man’s delay.

§5 In such anger as this did the army hold Archidamus during the halt. He, for his part, expecting — as it is said — that the Athenians would make some concession while their land was still whole, and would shrink from watching it once it had been cut up, held out.

Chapter 19

§1 When, however, though they assaulted Oenoe and tried every kind of approach, they could not take the place — and the Athenians, on their side, offered no parley — at last they broke away from it and invaded Attica, on about the eightieth day after the events at Plataea brought about by the Thebans who had entered, in summer, when the grain was at its height; and the commander was Archidamus son of Zeuxidamus, king of the Lacedaemonians.

§2 And being encamped, they began to cut up the land — first Eleusis and the Thriasian plain — and a certain falling-back of the Athenian horsemen near the pools called the Rheitus they brought about as well; then they pushed on, keeping Mount Aegaleum on their right hand, through the Cropia district, until they came to Acharnae, the largest place of those called demes in Attica; and settling themselves in it they made a camp, where, remaining a long time, they kept cutting up the land.

Chapter 20

§1 With such a resolve, it is said, Archidamus stayed on before Acharnae — drawn up as for battle — and did not come down into the plain on that invasion.

§2 For he hoped that the Athenians — at the height of a numerous youth and furnished for war as never before — would perhaps come out to meet him, and would not endure to watch while their land was being cut up.

§3 When, therefore, no Athenians met him at Eleusis and in the Thriasian plain, he sat down before Acharnae and made trial of whether they would come out.

§4 At the same time the place seemed to him suitable for encamping, and the Acharnians too — a great part of the city (for they were three thousand hoplites) — seemed to him not likely to look on while their own possessions were destroyed, but likely to press the whole people into battle. And if the Athenians did not come out even on that invasion, he could thereafter the more boldly both cut up the plain and advance against the very city; for the Acharnians, once deprived of what was theirs, would not be equally eager to run dangers for the property of the rest, and civil strife would enter into their counsels.

§5 Such was the thought in which Archidamus stayed before Acharnae.

Chapter 21

§1 The Athenians, so long as the army was about Eleusis and the Thriasian plain, cherished some hope that it would not come up any nearer to themselves — remembering also Pleistoanax son of Pausanias, king of the Lacedaemonians, who, having invaded Attica with an army of Peloponnesians, came as far as Eleusis and Thria fourteen years before the present war and then withdrew back again, having advanced no further (and it was from this cause, indeed, that his flight from Sparta came to pass, he being thought to have been bribed with money for the withdrawal).

§2 But when they saw the army at Acharnae, sixty stades distant from the city, they could no longer endure it: to them, as was natural, with their land being cut up before their eyes — a thing which the younger had never yet seen, nor the elders save in the Median war — it appeared a dreadful thing, and it seemed to the rest, and above all to the young men, that they should march out and not look on.

§3 Gathering in knots, they were in violent contention, some urging that they should march out, and certain others forbidding it. The oracle-mongers chanted oracles of every kind, whichever each man rushed to hear. And the Acharnians — thinking themselves no least share of the Athenians, when their own land it was that was being cut up — were the most vehement in urging the going out. By every means the city was being provoked to anger; they were angry with Pericles, and of his earlier counsels they remembered nothing at all, but reviled him because, being a general, he did not lead them out, and they held him responsible for all that they were suffering.

Chapter 22

§1 Pericles, seeing that for the moment they were exasperated and by no means thinking the best thoughts, but trusting that he judged aright in the question of not marching out, convened no assembly of theirs, nor any other gathering whatever, lest, coming together rather in wrath than in judgement, they should go astray; and he kept the city under guard, and held it in the greatest quietness he could.

§2 He did, however, keep sending out horsemen at all times, that no scouting parties might dash forth from the army to do damage to the fields near the city; and a brief cavalry skirmish took place in Phrygia between one squadron of the Athenian horse, with the Thessalians beside it, and the cavalry of the Boeotians, in which the Athenians and the Thessalians got not the worse of it, until the hoplites had come up in support of the Boeotians; then they turned to flight, and there fell of the Thessalians and the Athenians not many. The dead, however, they took up that same day, without a truce.

§3 And the Peloponnesians set up a trophy on the following day. This help of the Thessalians was rendered to the Athenians under an old alliance; and there came to their aid Larisaeans, Pharsalians, Parasians, Crannonians, Pyrasians, Gyrtonians and Pheraeans. Of these the commanders were: from Larisa, Polymedes and Aristanus, each from a different faction; and from Pharsalus, Menon; and the rest also had their leaders, each according to his city.

Chapter 23

§1 The Peloponnesians, since the Athenians did not come out against them to battle, broke up from Acharnae and ravaged certain other of the demes lying between Mount Parnes and Mount Brilessus.

§2 While they were yet in the land, the Athenians sent round the Peloponnese the hundred ships which they had been getting ready, and a thousand hoplites upon them, and four hundred archers; and the generals were Carcinus son of Xenotimus, and Proteas son of Epicles, and Socrates son of Antigenes.

§3 And these, putting out to sea with that armament, were sailing round; but the Peloponnesians, having stayed in Attica as long as their provisions served, withdrew through Boeotia, not by the way by which they had invaded; and, passing along by Oropus, they ravaged the land called Graea, which the Oropians, subjects of the Athenians, occupy; and, arriving in the Peloponnese, they broke up, each to his own city.

Chapter 24

§1 When the enemy had withdrawn, the Athenians established guards by land and by sea, which, as it proved, they were to maintain throughout the whole war; and it seemed good to them that a thousand talents from the monies in the acropolis, as a special reserve, should be set apart and not be spent, but that they should make war out of the other resources; and if anyone should propose or should vote to divert this money to any other use — unless the enemy should sail against the city with an army carried in ships and there should be need to defend it — they imposed death as the penalty.

§2 Along with the money they appointed each year a hundred of the best triremes, chosen out, and with them their trierarchs; and these were not to be used for any other purpose than together with the money, for the same danger, whenever the need should arise.

Chapter 25

§1 The Athenians who were sailing round the Peloponnese with the hundred ships — together with Corcyraeans who had come to their help with fifty ships, and certain others of the allies in those parts — were doing various mischief as they cruised; and putting in at Methone in Laconia they attacked the wall, which was weak and had no men in it.

§2 It chanced that about these regions Brasidas son of Tellis, a Spartan, was in command of a garrison; and, hearing of it, he brought help to the men in the place with a hundred hoplites; and, dashing through the Athenian camp — scattered as it was over the country, and faced towards the wall — he burst into Methone, and, losing but a few of his men in the rush, preserved the city; and for this piece of daring he was the first man in the war to be commended at Sparta.

§3 The Athenians weighed anchor and sailed along, and, putting in at Phia in Elis, they ravaged the land for two days, and defeated in battle the three hundred picked men who had come to the help from hollow Elis, and the Eleians of the perioecid district who dwelt there.

§4 When a great wind came down upon them, and they were being sorely battered in a place without a harbour, the greater part went aboard the ships and sailed round the cape called Ichthys into the harbour at Phia; while the Messenians meanwhile, and certain others who had not been able to get aboard, marched by land and took Phia.

§5 Later the ships, having sailed round, took them off, and they put out to sea, leaving Phia; for the greater part of the Eleian army was by now come up to the help. And the Athenians, sailing on to other places, ravaged the land.

Chapter 26

§1 About this same time the Athenians sent out thirty ships to keep watch, at one and the same time, upon Locris and Euboea; and their general was Cleopompus son of Cleinias.

§2 And, making descents, he ravaged part of the coastal country, and took Thronium, and received hostages from its people, and at Alope he defeated in battle the Locrians who had come to the rescue.

Chapter 27

§1 In this same summer the Athenians also drove the Aeginetans out of Aegina — themselves with their children and their women — charging them with being not least responsible for the war; and since Aegina lay close upon the Peloponnese, it seemed safer to hold it by sending out settlers of their own. Not long after they sent out the settlers to it.

§2 To the Aeginetans thus cast out the Lacedaemonians gave Thyrea to dwell in and land to till — in requital of their difference with the Athenians, and because the Aeginetans had been benefactors to them at the time of the earthquake and of the Helots’ revolt. The land of Thyrea is a border country between the Argive and the Laconian territory, reaching down to the sea. And some of them settled there, while the rest were scattered over the rest of Hellas.

Chapter 28

§1 In the same summer, on the day of new moon, by the moon’s reckoning — as indeed it seems possible for it to happen then only — the sun was eclipsed after midday, and was made full again, having become crescent-shaped, and certain of the stars appeared.

Chapter 29

§1 In the same summer the Athenians made Nymphodorus son of Pythes, a man of Abdera — whose sister Sitalces had in marriage, and who was a man of great influence with him — their proxenus, and sent for him, although they had before regarded him as an enemy; for they wished Sitalces son of Teres, king of the Thracians, to become their ally.

§2 This Teres, the father of Sitalces, was the first to make the great kingdom of the Odrysians spread over a greater part of the rest of Thrace; for a great part of the Thracians, even, is autonomous.

§3 This Teres has nothing to do with the Tereus who took to wife Procne the daughter of Pandion from Athens; nor were they even of the same Thrace: Tereus dwelt in Daulis, in the land now called Phocis, which was then inhabited by Thracians, and there the women did the deed concerning Itys (and with many of the poets also, at the mention of the nightingale, the bird has been named “Daulias”); and it is likely that Pandion drew this marriage-tie for his daughter over so great a distance for the sake of the advantage the parties had in one another, rather than for a road of many days to the Odrysians. Teres, who had not even the same name, was the first king to come into power among the Odrysians.

§4 Such a man as this the Athenians made their ally, wishing with his help to bring the places in the region of Thrace and Perdiccas over to their own side.

§5 Coming to Athens, Nymphodorus brought about the alliance of Sitalces, and made an Athenian of his son Sadocus; and he undertook to dissolve the war in the region of Thrace; for he would persuade Sitalces to send to the Athenians a Thracian army of horsemen and peltasts.

§6 He also brought Perdiccas over into agreement with the Athenians, and persuaded them to give Therma back to him; and Perdiccas at once took the field against the Chalcidians, together with the Athenians and Phormio.

§7 So Sitalces son of Teres, king of the Thracians, and Perdiccas son of Alexander, king of the Macedonians, became allies of the Athenians.

Chapter 30

§1 The Athenians in the hundred ships, while they were still cruising about the Peloponnese, took Sollion, a small town of the Corinthians, and gave it over to the Palaereans, alone of the Acarnanians, to occupy the land and city; and they took Astacus, of which Evarchus was tyrant, by force of arms, and, driving him out, attached the place to their alliance.

§2 And, sailing against the island of Cephallenia, they brought it over without a battle. Cephallenia lies over against Acarnania and Leucas, a land of four cities — Pala, Cranii, Same and Pronnae. Not long after, the ships withdrew to Athens.

Chapter 31

§1 About the autumn of this summer the Athenians, with their whole people, themselves and the metics, invaded the Megarid, Pericles son of Xanthippus being general. And the Athenians who were sailing round the Peloponnese in the hundred ships (for they happened by then to be at Aegina, on their way back home), as soon as they learned that the men from the city were in the Megarid with the whole levy, sailed to join them and united forces with them.

§2 This was in truth the greatest army of the Athenians gathered together in one body, the city being still at its height and not yet sickened; for the Athenians themselves were not fewer than ten thousand hoplites (apart from them they had the three thousand in Potidaea), and there took part in the invasion no fewer than three thousand hoplites of the metics, and besides them the rest of the throng of light-armed, which was not small. And after ravaging the greater part of the land they withdrew.

§3 And later in the war there took place other invasions also of the Athenians into the Megarid, year by year, both with their horsemen and in full levy, until Nisaea was taken by the Athenians.

Chapter 32

§1 Atalanta also was fortified by the Athenians as a fort towards the very end of this summer — the island that lies off the Opuntian Locrians, which had been desert before — so that pirates might not sail out from Opus and the rest of Locris and work harm upon Euboea. These things were done in this summer, after the Peloponnesians had withdrawn from Attica.

Chapter 33

§1 In the winter that followed, Evarchus the Acarnanian, wishing to come back to Astacus, persuaded the Corinthians to sail with forty ships and five hundred and a thousand hoplites and bring him back; and he himself besides hired some mercenaries. The command of the force was held by Euphamidas son of Aristonymus, Timoxenus son of Timocrates, and Eumachus son of Chrysis.

§2 And they sailed and brought him back; and of the rest of Acarnania, of the parts that lie along the sea, there are certain places which they wished to attach to themselves, and, attempting them, since they could not, they put out to sea for home.

§3 Putting in on their way along the coast to Cephallenia and making a landing in the territory of the Cranians, they were deceived by the Cranians under the colour of a certain agreement; and, the Cranians attacking them unawares, they lost some men of their own, and, putting out to sea again with more of haste and violence, they were brought home.

Chapter 34

§1 In this same winter the Athenians, following the ancestral law, performed the public burial of the first of those who had fallen in this war, in the manner following.

§2 The bones of the departed are laid out three days beforehand, a tent having been set up, and each man brings to his own dead whatever offering he pleases;

§3 When the bodies are brought forth, wagons carry chests of cypress wood, one for each tribe, and in them lie the bones of the tribe to which each of the dead belonged; and one bier, empty, is carried, decked out for the missing — for those, that is, who could not be found for the recovery of their bodies.

§4 And anyone who wishes, citizen or foreigner alike, may join in the bringing out of the body; and the women who are of kin to the dead are present at the tomb, making lament.

§5 They lay them, then, in the public tomb, which stands in the most beautiful suburb of the city; and there they always bury those who have died in the wars — all save those at Marathon: judging their valour to be preeminent, they made their tomb on the very spot.

§6 When they have hidden them in the earth, a man chosen by the city, such as is thought not unintelligent in judgement and to stand high in repute, pronounces over them the eulogy that is fitting; and after this they depart.

§7 In such manner they bury. And throughout the whole war, whenever the occasion befell them, they observed the law.

§8 It was for these first fallen, then, that Pericles son of Xanthippus was chosen to speak; and when the moment came, stepping forward from the tomb onto a platform built high, so that he might be heard by as much of the throng as possible, he spoke as follows.

Chapter 35

§1 Most of those who have already spoken here praise the man who added this oration to the law, on the ground that it is a fine thing that a speech should be delivered over those who are buried of the wars; but to me it would have seemed enough that men who have proved themselves good in deed should have the honours shown them likewise in deed — such as you now see given publicly around this tomb — and not that the virtues of many should be put in hazard, to be trusted to one man, whether he speaks them well or worse.

§2 For it is hard to speak with measure in a matter where even the semblance of truth is scarcely established. The hearer who knows the facts and is well disposed would perhaps suppose that the things being set forth fall short of what he both desires and knows; while to the hearer who knows nothing of them there will be points at which he even takes the speech to be exaggerated — out of envy — whenever he hears anything beyond his own nature. For praises of others are tolerable so far — so long as each man thinks himself capable in his own person of doing some part of what he hears; but against him who surpasses these they are already envious and disbelieving.

§3 Since, then, it has been sanctioned of old that these things are rightly done in this way, I too must needs, following the law, strive to win from you the goodwill of each man and his judgement, so far as may be.

Chapter 36

§1 I shall begin from the forefathers first: for it is just, and at the same time fitting, that they be given this tribute of remembrance on an occasion such as this. For the same men it was who, dwelling ever in the land, handed it down through successive generations even to this day, and delivered it free through their valour.

§2 And they are worthy of praise, and still more our fathers; for they won, over and above the inheritance they received, as great an empire as we now hold, and left it, not without toil, to us who live today.

§3 And of that empire the greater part we ourselves, the present generation, have enlarged — being still mostly in the prime of life; and we have furnished the city with all things, most self-sufficient alike for war and for peace.

§4 Of these things, the deeds of war, by which each several possession was acquired — whatever further we or our fathers defended with ready spirit against a barbarian or a Greek enemy that came against us — these I shall pass over, not meaning to speak at length before men who know them; but from what manner of training we advanced to them, and under what form of constitution, and out of what ways of life so great a greatness has arisen, these things I shall set forth first, and then go on to the praise of these men; for I judge that at the present moment it would not be unseemly for these things to be said, and that it is profitable for the whole throng — citizens and foreigners together — to hear them out.

Chapter 37

§1 For ours is a constitution that does not copy the laws of our neighbours; we are ourselves rather a pattern to some than imitators of others. And its name, because the state is administered not for the few but for the many, is a democracy; while at law all share alike in the settlement of their private differences. And as to standing, according as each man is in repute in something, so is the greater advancement in public life accorded him — by worth, and not by faction-membership; nor yet, on the score of poverty, is a man who has it in him to do the city some good kept back by the obscurity of his standing.

§2 We take our part in public life freely; and free, too, is our bearing with one another amid all the suspicion that besets the ways of every day: we do not keep an angry eye upon our neighbour if he follow his own liking, nor add to our intercourse with him those sour looks of displeasure which, though no law can touch them, are galling to the face.

§3 Turning to our own concerns without constraint, we yet do not transgress what is public — and that chiefly through fear, in obedience to those who are ever in office and to the laws, and above all to those of the laws which are laid down for the help of the wronged, and to those which, though unwritten, bring a shame that all men allow.

Chapter 38

§1 Moreover, from our toils we have provided for the mind very many recreations — games and sacrifices held the whole year through, as the custom is, and in private life a graceful furnishing of things, whose delight of every day drives out what is irksome in them.

§2 And through the greatness of our city there flows in from all the earth everything; and it comes about for us that the goods the land itself produces are enjoyed by us with no more familiar a delight than the goods of all other men.

Chapter 39

§1 And in the training for war we are different from our opponents in these ways. For we leave our city open to all the world, and we never by expulsions of aliens bar anyone from any lesson or spectacle — from any such thing, that is, as, were it not hidden, an enemy might profit by seeing; for our trust is placed not so much in preparations and craft as in the dauntless spirit which we ourselves bring to the deeds; and in our schooling, while others pursue courage from their earliest youth by laborious exercise, we, living at our ease, advance none the less against the evenly matched dangers.

§2 There is proof of this: the Lacedaemonians do not come against our land by themselves alone — with all their allies they make their invasion — while we, going ourselves against our neighbours’ land, and fighting there on soil not ours against men who defend their own property, for the most part gain the mastery without difficulty.

§3 Against our strength gathered in one body no enemy has ever yet engaged, because of the care we give at once to the fleet and to the sending of ourselves by land in many directions; but whenever they engage some part of us, then, having got the better of some of our men, they boast that all of us have been driven from the field — and, when they are beaten, that it was by the whole of us that they were worsted.

§4 And yet, if ours is a will to face peril rather by way of ease than by laborious training, and not so much by the help of laws as by a habit of courage, we gain by it this: not to be worn out in advance under the hardships that are still before us, and, when we are come upon them, to show ourselves no fainter than men whose lives are one long toil among them — and that in these things our city is worthy of admiration, and in others besides.

Chapter 40

§1 For we are lovers of the beautiful with frugality, and we pursue wisdom without softness. We use wealth for the occasion of action rather than for the boast of words; and poverty is for a man no disgrace to confess — the deeper disgrace is not to escape it in deed.

§2 In the same men there is care at once for their own affairs and for the city’s; and in others, whose bent is towards their own business, there is no scant knowledge of public affairs. For we alone of all reckon the man who takes no share in these things not a man of harmless quiet but a useless man; and we are the same men whether we are deciding public matters or deliberating upon them, and this we do rightly — not holding that words are a damage to deeds, but deeming the greater damage to come to the doing of what is needful without having been schooled beforehand in words.

§3 And in this too we stand apart, that the very same men are at once boldest in daring and most given to reckoning beforehand what we are to attempt; whereas with others it is ignorance that brings boldness and calculation that brings hesitancy. And most justly would those be adjudged best in spirit who know both the grievous and the pleasant things most clearly, and are not by these things deterred from their dangers.

§4 And in the things that concern virtue we are at variance with the many: for we win our friends not by receiving good but by conferring it. And the doer of the favour is the firmer friend, keeping alive, by the continuing goodwill he bears to the man he has benefited, the gratitude that is owed him; while he who owes his counter-service is the duller in friendship, knowing that when he repays the kindness, it will be paid not as a free gift but as a debt.

§5 And we alone do good to a man fearlessly, not more by a reckoning of profit than by the confidence of freedom.

Chapter 41

§1 And summing all together, I say that our whole city is the schooling of Hellas; and one by one, it seems to me, the same man among us could know most things, and with the utmost grace and the readiest versatility could present himself, his own body self-sufficient.

§2 And that this is for the present no vaunting of words but a truth of deeds, the very power of the city makes plain — the power which from these ways of ours we have won.

§3 For alone among the cities of today it comes to the proof greater than the report of it; and alone it affords neither to the enemy who comes against it any ground of indignation at the sort of men whose hands afflict him, nor to the subject any ground of complaint that he is under men not worthy.

§4 Furnishing our power with great signs, and assuredly not without witness, we shall be admired by the men of today and by those of aftertime; and we stand in need of no Homer for our praiser, nor of any other who with his verses would give pleasure for the moment, while the truth will damage the conception he gives of the deeds; but rather, having compelled every sea and all land to lie open to our daring, we have planted everywhere imperishable memorials, of evil and of good alike.

§5 For such a city, then, these men too, judging it nobly just not to be robbed of her, died fighting; and of those who are left behind it is to be expected that each and all will be willing to toil on her behalf.

Chapter 42

§1 For this reason it is that I have drawn out the matter of the city: to give a lesson — that the struggle is not on equal terms for us and for those to whom nothing of all this belongs in like measure — and at the same time to set the reasonableness of what I now say out in the open, upon signs.

§2 And the greatest things of hers have now been said: for the praise I sang of the city received its adornment from the virtues of these men and of their like; and among the Greeks there are not many in whom word would so evenly balance against deeds as in these men. And the present ending of these men seems to me to point out a man’s virtue — first making it known, and last confirming it.

§3 For even to men worse in all else it is right to give the first place to the manhood they have shown in the wars for their fatherland; for by blotting out evil with good they profited the common good more than they harmed it through their own failings.

§4 And of these men not one was made soft by setting before all else the further enjoyment of his wealth, nor did any in the hope of escaping poverty — on the thought that, if he escaped it, he would yet grow rich — make the dread thing a matter to put off; but holding the chastisement of their enemies a thing more longed for than their own selves, and deeming this danger at once the most beautiful of dangers, they chose with it to chasten the one and to strive after the other — committing to hope the unseen issues of success, but in deed, as touching what was already before their own eyes, deeming it worthy to trust in themselves; and deeming it better, in the very act of defence, to suffer than by yielding to be saved, they fled the shame that lies in words and endured the deed in their own bodies; and in the briefest turn of fortune, at once at the high point of glory rather than of fear, they were set free.

Chapter 43

§1 And these men became such men as it was fitting the city should have; the rest must pray for what is safer, but must not therefore hold their own spirit against the enemy worthy to be one whit fainter — considering the advantage not in word only, the advantage which anyone might set out at length to you who know it none the worse, telling of all the good things there are in standing against the enemy; but rather beholding day by day the power of the city in deed, and growing into lovers of her, and, whenever she seems to you great, bethinking you that men won this greatness by daring, by knowing what was needful, and by feeling shame in their deeds; and when at any time they failed in some trial, they did not on that account think fit to rob the city of the excellence that was proper to them, but cast before her the fairest of contributions.

§2 For giving their bodies in the common cause, they received each for himself a praise that does not grow old, and the most signal of tombs — not so much the tomb wherein they lie as the one wherein their glory is left behind, a memory that shall not die, ready to every man who comes upon it at each occasion of word and deed.

§3 For the whole earth is the tomb of famous men; and not on the columns in their own land alone does the inscription tell of them — in a land not theirs also there dwells, in every man, an unwritten memory — rather of their spirit than of their deed.

§4 Emulating them now, and judging that the blessed is the free, and the free the brave of heart, do not turn your eyes away from the dangers of war.

§5 For it is not the men whose fortune is grievous — those in whom no hope of good any longer lives — who could with more justice grow careless of their lives; but those in whom, while they live, the contrary turn still hangs in the balance, and in whom most is at stake, should they stumble anywhere.

§6 For to a man of high spirit the suffering that goes with being unmanned is more grievous than the death that comes all-unfelt, together with strength and the common hope.

Chapter 44

§1 For this reason it is that to the parents of these men — to those of you present — I shall bring consolations rather than laments. For they have been bred amid mischances of every kind, and know them; and blessed is the fortune of those upon whom the fairest share befalls — as now upon these men, in their dying, upon you, in your grieving — and for whom the life was measured out alike, both for living in blessedness and for ending in it.

§2 Hard, I know, is the persuading, such being the case; for in others’ good fortunes — those in which once you also exulted — you will often have reminders of your own; and pain is not of the blessings a man is stripped of without ever proving them, but of that whose wonted use is snatched away.

§3 They must be steadfast besides, in the hope of other children — for those to whom their age still gives the begetting of them; for the children who come after will be to some, in their private life, a forgetting of those who are gone, and the city will profit from two quarters, both from not being left desolate and from safety. For it is not possible for anything equal or just to be counselled where men do not also stake their own children upon the same hazards.

§4 And as many of you as have passed beyond that age — count it gain, the more of a life you have lived in happiness, and count what remains to you brief; and be lightened by the glory of these men. For love of honour alone is without old age; and when the time of life is past its use, it is not, as some men say, the getting of gain that delights the more, but the being honoured.

Chapter 45

§1 And to the children of these men, in their turn — as many of you as are present — and to their brothers, I see that the contest before you is great (for the man who is present no more all the world is wont to praise); and scarcely, even by an excellence beyond all measure, would you be judged their equals — nay, a little their inferiors. For envy attends the living in his rivalry; but the man who no longer stands in the way is honoured with goodwill that none contests.

§2 And if I must make mention also of some part of womanly excellence — for the women who will now be left in widowhood — I shall set it all out with a brief word of counsel. For great in you women is the glory — not to fall short of the nature that is in you; and the renown is hers of whom there is least speech among the men, whether for praise or for blame.

Chapter 46

§1 What it was mine to say has now been said, in speech, according to the law; and in deed the men buried here have already received their adornment in part, while of the rest that is theirs their children the city will bring up from this day forth at the public charge until manhood — holding out a crown of profit to these children and to all who are left behind of such contests. For where the prizes set before excellence are greatest, there too the best men bear their part in the life of the city.

§2 Now, having made your lament, each what is due to him, depart.

Chapter 47

§1 Such was the burial that was made in this winter; and when the winter was past, the first year of this war was brought to its end.

§2 At the very beginning of the following summer, the Peloponnesians and their allies invaded Attica, with two-thirds of their number, as they had done also at the first (and the commander was Archidamus son of Zeuxidamus, king of the Lacedaemonians); and, settling down in the land, they ravaged it.

§3 They had been yet but a few days in Attica when the sickness first began to come upon the Athenians. It was said to have broken out also before this in many places, both about Lemnos and in other lands; but nowhere was there remembered so great a pestilence, nor so great a destruction of mankind.

§4 For the physicians, at the first, treating it in ignorance, could not avail — nay, they themselves died the most, for that they most came near the sick; nor did any other human art of any kind avail; and whatever they sought as suppliants at the sanctuaries, or whatever use they made of oracles and such-like, it was all unavailing; and at the last they drew off from them, worsted by the calamity.

Chapter 48

§1 The sickness began first, as men say, from Ethiopia, the country above Egypt; then it came down also into Egypt and into Libya, and into the greater part of the King’s land.

§2 It fell upon the city of the Athenians suddenly; and first it laid hold of the people in the Piraeus, so that it was even said by them that the Peloponnesians had cast poisons into the wells — for there were as yet no springs in that place. Later it came also into the upper city, and now men died far more.

§3 Let each man speak of it, then, as he knows it, physician and layman alike — from what cause it was likely to have arisen, and what causes he deems sufficient in power to bring about so great a change. But what the sickness was like as it came, I shall tell; and those things, from which a man, if he marked them, would best have foreknowledge and so not be at a loss, if ever it should come down upon us again — these I shall set out, I who have myself fallen sick of it and have myself watched others suffering.

Chapter 49

§1 For the year, as was generally agreed, was of all others the freest from sickness — that is, as regarded the other maladies; and whatever a man chanced to be suffering from before, it was into this that everything resolved.

§2 The others it took with no manifest occasion, but suddenly — men who until then were in health: first there were strong heats in the head, and redness and burning of the eyes; and the parts within — throat and tongue — were straightway suffused with blood, and the breath given off was strange and noisome.

§3 After that, sneezing and hoarseness came on, and within no long time the pain descended into the chest, attended by a violent cough; and when it settled upon the heart, it threw it into disorder, and all the discharges of bile that have been named by physicians came on besides, and these with great distress.

§4 Upon most, too, there came retching that brought nothing up, with a strong convulsion; and with some this abated soon after, with others not till much later.

§5 Outwardly, to the touch, the body was neither excessively hot nor yet pale, but of a dusky red, livid, breaking out in small blisters and in sores; but the parts within burned so that the sick could not endure the laying-on even of the very thinnest garments and of fine linen, nor anything else save to go naked; and there was nothing so welcome to them as to throw themselves into cold water. And many of those who had none to attend them did even this, throwing themselves into the wells, mastered by a thirst that would not cease; and drinking much or drinking little, it was all one to them.

§6 The inability to lie still and the sleeplessness pressed upon them the whole time through. And the body, for as long a time as the sickness was at its height, did not waste away, but — beyond expectation — withstood the distress, so that the most were destroyed on the ninth day or on the seventh by the inward burning, still having some strength left; or, if they escaped that, with the sickness coming down upon the belly and a strong ulceration arising there and at the same time a sheer flux coming upon them, the majority were later carried off through it by weakness.

§7 For the mischief, having first established its seat in the head, passed right through the whole body, beginning from above; and in any man who came safely out of the greatest perils, it was by the seizure of his extremities that it gave token of itself.

§8 For it settled upon the privy parts, and upon the tips of hands and feet; and many, robbed of these, yet came through with life; and there were some who lost their eyes also. And upon some forgetfulness laid hold, directly they had risen up, of all things alike; and they knew neither their own selves nor their own people.

Chapter 50

§1 For — the very fashion of the sickness being a thing beyond all words — it fell in every other respect too more grievously upon each man than accords with the nature of man; and in this above all it made plain that it was something other than any of the kinds men have grown up with: for all the birds and four-footed beasts, whatsoever of them are wont to touch men — with the many lying unburied — either came not near at all, or, if they tasted, were destroyed.

§2 And a proof of it: of birds of that sort there was a manifest disappearance — they were seen nowhere at all, neither elsewhere nor about anything of the kind; but the dogs rather gave indication of what was coming to pass, in that they share the life of men.

Chapter 51

§1 The sickness, then, was throughout of this fashion, in its whole character — passing over many another strange feature, each case chancing to differ for one man from another; and at that season no other of the wonted ailments troubled them; but whatever did chance to arise, in this it came to its end.

§2 Men died, some through neglect, others even with the very utmost of tendance; and there was established, so to say, no one single remedy — not one — such that, once applied, it must needs benefit; for what had agreed with one man, this same thing harmed another.

§3 And no body, being in itself sufficient, was seen to avail against the sickness, whether in strength or in weakness; but all alike it carried off, and those too that were tended with every care of regimen.

§4 But the most dreadful thing of all the evil was the despair whenever a man perceived himself sickening (for, turning at once in mind to the hopeless, they gave themselves up far the more, nor did they hold out), and that in the tendance of one another they were filled with the sickness, one man from another, and died like sheep; and this was what begat the greatest destruction.

§5 For if, fearing, they would not come near to one another, they perished in their solitude; and many houses were emptied for want of one to tend them; but if they did come near, they were destroyed — and that most of all in the case of such as laid claim to some share of virtue; for out of shame they spared not their own selves in going in to their friends, since even the members of the household, carrying through to the end the laments for those who died away, were worn out, worsted by the greatness of the evil.

§6 Yet the more did those who had escaped pity the dying and the afflicted, for that they knew beforehand and were by now themselves of good courage; for on the same man it laid hold never twice, so as even to kill him. And they were pronounced blessed by the others, and they themselves had some light hope — in the instant overflowing of their joy, and for the time thereafter — that never again would they be destroyed by any other sickness whatever.

Chapter 52

§1 And the gathering in from the fields into the town pressed them yet more besides the toil already upon them, and it pressed no less upon those who had come in after.

§2 For no houses were to be had, but they were quartered in stifling huts at that season of the year; and the destruction came on without any sort of order; but the dead, one upon another as they died, lay; and they tumbled in the roads; and half-dead they lay about all the springs in their desire for the water.

§3 And the sanctuaries in which they had quartered themselves were full of dead bodies, of men dying on the very spot; for as the evil bore them down beyond bearing, and they had nothing that they might do, they fell into carelessness towards things sacred and things secular alike.

§4 And all the customs that they had been used to observe concerning burials were confounded, and each buried the dead as he was able; and many had recourse to shameless interments for want of the necessaries, because of the many of their own who had already died. For, arriving at the pyres of others before those who had furnished them, some set their own dead upon them and kindled them; and others, when another’s body was burning, cast upon the top the dead they carried, and so went away.

Chapter 53

§1 The sickness was the first beginning, for the city, of a yet greater lawlessness in the other things of life as well; for men more readily dared the things that before they had hidden — things done not for pleasure — seeing the reversal come swift upon men: the prosperous dying off suddenly, and men who till then had never had anything at once in possession of their goods.

§2 So they held it meet to make their winnings quick and turn them to present enjoyment, holding alike that body and goods were but for a day.

§3 And no man was forward to take toil upon himself for what bore the appearance of nobility, doubtful as each held it whether he should live to come at it; while what was at once pleasant, and gainful on every side withal, this it was that stood both for noble and for expedient.

§4 Neither the fear of the gods nor the law of men held any man back. To reverence, or not to reverence, they judged all one, seeing all men perishing alike; and for transgressions no one hoped to live to pay the penalty, since what was already adjudged against them hung over them by far the heavier — which, before it fell upon them, it was but natural to get some enjoyment of life.

Chapter 54

§1 The Athenians, falling into such an affliction, were hard pressed, with men dying within and the land being ravaged without.

§2 In the evil, as was like enough, they called to mind also this verse, the elder men affirming that it had been sung of old: “A Dorian war shall come, and a pestilence with it.”

§3 A dispute arose then among the people — that by the men of old it was not a pestilence that had been named in the verse, but a famine; but for the present, and reasonably, it prevailed to say a pestilence; for men adjusted their remembering to what they suffered. But if ever, I suppose, another Dorian war later than this shall befall, and famine chance to arise in its stead, in all likelihood they will sing it so.

§4 And those who knew of it remembered also the Lacedaemonians’ oracle — how at the time when, they asking the god whether it was needful to make war, he gave answer that to those fighting with all their might the victory would be, and he himself, he said, would join to help.

§5 Concerning the oracle, men judged what was coming to pass to be according to it: when the Peloponnesians had invaded, the sickness began immediately; and it entered not into the Peloponnese — which also was worth the telling — but lighted most of all upon Athens, and after that upon the most populous of the other places besides. Such were the things that befell concerning the sickness.

Chapter 55

§1 The Peloponnesians, when they had cut up the plain, passed over into the land called Paralos, as far as Laurium, where the silver mines of the Athenians are; and they cut up first the side of it which looks toward the Peloponnese, and then that which is turned toward Euboea and Andros.

§2 Pericles was general also at that time, and as for not marching the Athenians out, he held the same opinion as in the former invasion.

Chapter 56

§1 Yet while the Peloponnesians were still in the plain, before they came down upon the coast, he was making ready a descent of a hundred ships upon the Peloponnese; and when all was ready, he put out to sea.

§2 And he carried aboard the ships four thousand hoplites of the Athenians and three hundred horsemen, in transports for the horses, then for the first time made from the old ships; and the Chians and the Lesbians joined the expedition besides, with fifty ships.

§3 When this armament of the Athenians put out to sea, they left the Peloponnesians still in Attica, upon the coast.

§4 Arriving at Epidaurus of the Peloponnese, they cut up most of the land; and coming to the assault of the city, they were brought into hope of taking it — but it went no further.

§5 And putting out to sea again from Epidaurus, they cut up the land of Troezen, and Halieis, and Hermione; and all these places are upon the sea-coast of the Peloponnese.

§6 And sailing from thence, they came to Prasiae, a small coast-town of Laconia; and they cut up its land, and took and sacked the very town. This done, they sailed for home; and the Peloponnesians they found no longer in Attica, but already withdrawn.

Chapter 57

§1 All the while that the Peloponnesians were in the land of the Athenians, and the Athenians were campaigning with their ships, the sickness was wearing down the Athenians both in the armament and in the city; so that it was even said that the Peloponnesians, struck with fear of the sickness — as they learned from the deserters that it was in the city, and were aware at the same time that they were burying their dead — went out of the land the sooner.

§2 It was in this invasion that they remained longest of all, and they cut up the whole land; for they were in the Attic land about forty days.

Chapter 58

§1 The same summer, Hagnon son of Nicias and Cleopompus son of Cleinias, being fellow-generals with Pericles, took the very armament with which he had made his expedition, and marched at once against the Chalcidians in Thrace and against Potidaea, which was still under siege; and arriving there, they brought their engines to bear upon Potidaea, and tried by every means to take it.

§2 But for them neither did the taking of the city go forward worthily, nor anything else of their preparations; for the sickness arising there pressed very hard upon the Athenians, destroying the army — so that even the Athenian soldiers of the earlier levy fell sick from the army that had come with Hagnon, men who up to that time had been in health. Phormio, however, and the six hundred and the thousand were no longer about the Chalcidians.

§3 Hagnon, accordingly, sailed back with his ships to Athens, having lost to the sickness, of his four thousand hoplites, a thousand and fifty in about forty days; and the soldiers of the earlier levy, remaining where they were, kept up the siege of Potidaea.

Chapter 59

§1 After the second invasion of the Peloponnesians, the Athenians, with their land cut up for the second time and the sickness lying hard upon them together with the war, were changing their resolve,

§2 they held Pericles responsible, as the man who had persuaded them to the war and through whom they had fallen into their misfortunes; and they were eager to come to terms with the Lacedaemonians; and sending some envoys to them, they accomplished nothing. Being at a loss in every quarter of their minds, they set upon Pericles.

§3 He, seeing them exasperated by the present troubles and doing just all that he had himself expected, called an assembly together (for he was still general), and was minded to encourage them and, leading away the anger of their minds, to bring them to a gentler and more unafraid temper; and coming forward he spoke as follows.

Chapter 60

§1 And to me, who was expecting it, this anger of yours has come to pass upon me (for I am aware of the causes); and it is for this very cause that I have called the assembly together — that I may remind you, and put forth my complaint, if in any respect you are not in the right either in your exasperation against me or in your yielding to your misfortunes.

§2 For I hold that a city being set upright in its whole self does more good to the private men than the faring-well of each citizen apart, while the city as a whole founders.

§3 For a man who fares well in his own affairs, while his fatherland is being destroyed, is none the less destroyed together with it; while one faring ill in a fatherland that fares well is far the more preserved.

§4 Since, then, the city is able to bear the misfortunes that are its own, but each man singly is unable to bear those that befall it, how is it not the duty of all to stand to its defence — and not to do the things you now do? For, dismayed by the misfortunes at home, you abandon the common deliverance; and both me, who advised you to make war, and yourselves, who joined in consenting, you hold in blame.

§5 And yet upon me, upon such a man, your anger is bent — who, so I judge, am inferior to none in discerning what is needful and in expounding it, who am a lover of this city, and stronger than money.

§6 For he who knows but cannot clearly teach is in the same case as if he had not considered at all; and he who has both, but is ill-affected toward the city, would not equally speak aught that was for the city’s own interest; and if this too be present, but he be overcome by money, the whole together would be sold for this one thing.

§7 So that if, deeming that these things belonged to me more than to others — even if but in middling measure — you were yet persuaded by me to make war, it is not reasonable that I should now bear the blame of having done wrong.

Chapter 61

§1 For even to those to whom choice has come to be, while all else fares well with them, it is great folly to make war; but if it was necessary either to yield at once and obey those over against them, or to run the risk and prevail, the man who fled the danger is more to blame than the man who stood it.

§2 And I am the same man, and I do not change; but you change, since it befell you to be persuaded while your fortunes were yet unharmed, and to repent when you were suffering harm; and my argument appears not right to the weakness of your resolve — because the painful thing is already present to the perception of each man, while of the benefit the demonstration is still absent to all; and, with so great a change befallen you, and this out of a little space, your spirit is cast low, powerless to hold out in what you had resolved.

§3 For the sudden and the unlooked-for enslaves the spirit, and that which befalls with the greatest contrariety to reason; and this, with the rest, has befallen you not least in the sickness.

§4 Yet, dwelling in a great city and bred in ways that match it, you must be willing to stand up under even the greatest misfortunes and not to efface its claim (for men judge with an even hand, both to blame whoever falls short, through softness, of the repute he has to begin with, and to hate him who grasps with insolence at what is not befitting), and, when your private losses have ceased to give pain, to lay hold of the common deliverance.

Chapter 62

§1 As for the toil that waits upon the war — your fear lest it prove great and that we prevail none the more — let those things suffice you in which I many a time before demonstrated that the mistrust of it was not just; and I will make clear this besides, which I think neither you yourselves have ever yet thought upon — the greatness you possess with respect to your rule — nor I in my former speeches; and not even now would I have employed a more vainglorious pretension, had I not seen you dismayed beyond what was likely.

§2 For you think you are rulers over your allies alone; I declare that of the two manifest parts that serve for use — land, and sea — you are paramount masters of the whole of the one, both so far as you now hold it and further yet, should you wish; and there is no one who will stop you, with the naval armament you possess, from sailing — neither the King nor any other nation of those now in being.

§3 So that this power does not show itself in the use of houses and land, of which you think the loss so great; nor is it reasonable to bear their loss with painfulness more than, on the reckoning that beside this power they are not so much as a garden-plot and an ornament of wealth, to hold them in slight regard; and to know that freedom, if we take hold of it and bring it safe through, will easily take these things up again, while for those who have obeyed others it is the way that even what was won before grows less; and to show yourselves in both respects no worse than your fathers — they, with toil, and not receiving it at other hands, both held their own and moreover, bringing it safe through, handed it on to you (it is a baser thing to be robbed of what one holds than to fail in the winning); and to go against the enemies not with high spirit only, but with contempt besides.

§4 For a vaunting display is born of the ignorance of the fortunate man and of a certain cowardice; but disdain is the mark of him who is confident by judgement that he prevails over his opposites — and this is what we possess.

§5 And the boldness that comes of like fortune, the understanding bred of the consciousness of superiority provides more firmly founded; and such boldness trusts less to hope — the strength of which shows itself in the strait — than to a judgement grounded upon the things at hand, of which the foresight is the more assured.

Chapter 63

§1 It is fitting that you help the city in that wherewith it is honoured through its rule — that wherein all of you exult — and not to flee the toils of it, or else to forgo the pursuit of the honours of it; nor yet to think that you are contending over one thing only, slavery in place of freedom, but over the loss of your rule besides, and over dangers from the things wherein in your rule you have drawn hatred down upon you.

§2 Of which rule you can no longer stand aside, even if someone besides, for fear, would at this present time make a display of valour out of doing nothing; for you hold it now as a tyranny — which it may seem unjust to have taken, but is dangerous to let go.

§3 Such men would most speedily destroy a city, if they persuaded others, and if anywhere they settled by themselves, autonomous, would destroy it no less; for unenterprising quiet does not hold its own unless it be marshalled side by side with the enterprising, nor does it profit in a city that rules, but in one that is subject — to be slaves in safety.

Chapter 64

§1 But do not be swept away by citizens of that sort, nor hold me in anger — with whom you yourselves also joined in the resolve to make war — even if the enemy, having come on, have done what was to be expected towards men who would not obey them; and this sickness has befallen us beyond what we looked for, the only thing of all that has come to pass greater than hope. And I know that through it I am hated yet more — unjustly, unless, whenever you meet with some good beyond reckoning, you will set that also to my credit.

§2 What comes of the divine must be borne under necessity, and what comes of the enemy, with courage; for this was the wont of this city in time past, and let it not now be cut off in you.

§3 Know that it has the greatest name among all men, because it does not yield to its misfortunes, having spent in war lives and toil beyond all others, and having gained to this day the greatest power; of which, even if we now at some time yield (for all things are by nature wont also to grow less), there will be left behind to those who come after a memory unto eternity: that Greeks, we ruled over most Greeks, and stood firm in the greatest wars, against all together and against each one by one; and have made our homes in the city of all others most abundant and greatest.

§4 And yet, over these things, the unenterprising man might find fault; the man who is minded himself to be doing something will emulate them; and whoever possesses none of them will grudge them.

§5 To be hated and to be grievous to men for the present hour — that has been the portion of all who have judged that others should be ruled over by others; but whoever takes the envy upon himself in the greatest affairs takes right counsel. For hatred does not hold out for long; and the brilliance of the moment, and thereafter glory ever-remembered, is left behind.

§6 Do you, foreseeing for the time to come the noble, and for the present the not-shameful, make both your own with the eagerness now at hand; and neither open parley with the Lacedaemonians nor show yourselves overborne by the toils at hand, as being of those who are least distressed in their minds by misfortunes and hold out against them most in deed — for such are the best, both of cities and of private men.

Chapter 65

§1 Pericles, saying words such as these, was trying to dissolve the anger of the Athenians towards himself, and to turn away their thoughts from the misfortunes at hand.

§2 In public they were won over by his words, and no longer sent to the Lacedaemonians, and were already the more set upon the war; but in private they were grieved by their sufferings — the common people, because, having set out from little, they had been stripped even of that; the men of power, because they had lost fair estates in the country, together with their buildings and their costly furnishings; and above all, that they had war instead of peace.

§3 Yet not even so did the whole people cease to hold him in anger before they had punished him with a fine.

§4 But a little while after — as a crowd is wont to do — they chose him general, and entrusted all their affairs to him: for touching the things in which each man was smarting in private, they were by now less keenly sensible; and touching what the whole city needed, they judged it of the greatest worth.

§5 For throughout so long a time as he stood at the head of the city in time of peace, he guided it moderately and guarded it safely, and it became greatest under him; and when the war had set in, he appears here too to have foreseen its power.

§6 He lived on two years and six months; and when he died, his foresight concerning the war was recognized yet more fully.

§7 For he said that, if they kept quiet, and tended the fleet, and added nothing to their rule in the course of the war, and brought the city into no danger, they would prevail; but they did the very opposite of all this, and in other things besides, which seemed to lie outside the war, they managed the commonwealth according to private love of honour and private gains, to the hurt both of themselves and of the allies — things which, when they went well, were to the private men rather honour and advantage; and when they failed, became a damage to the city in the war.

§8 The reason was that he, being powerful through his standing and his judgement, and being besides, in the matter of money, most conspicuously unbribable, held the multitude in liberty, and was not more led by it than he himself led it: because he did not win influence from any improper quarter, he did not speak to their pleasure, but, holding his place on the strength of his worth, he could stand against them even in anger.

§9 Whenever therefore he perceived them bold out of season, in insolence, he would strike them down by his words into fear; and when in turn they were afraid without reason, he would restore them again to boldness. And it came about that in word it was a democracy, but in deed the rule of the first man.

§10 But the men who came after, being more upon a level one with another, and each striving to become first, turned to gratifying the people and to surrendering the conduct of affairs into its hands.

§11 Out of which many other things miscarried, as was to be looked for in a great city that holds rule, and among them the voyage to Sicily — which was not so much a failure of judgement in respect of those against whom they sailed, as on the part of the senders, who did not judge the measures that were fitting for the men who had gone out, but, out of their private slanders over the leadership of the commons, both made the business in the camp more feeble and first embroiled the affairs of the city among themselves.

§12 Beaten in Sicily, in another armament besides and in the greater part of their fleet, and now in civil war at home, they none the less held out three years against the enemies they had from the first, and against those from Sicily together with them, and against most of the allies now in revolt, and, when Cyrus the King’s son had later been added to their foes — he who furnished money to the Peloponnesians for the fleet — they did not give in before they had fallen in among their own private differences and been overthrown.

§13 So much surplus was there to Pericles at that time, beyond the things he himself had foreseen — that the city would prevail very easily over none but the Peloponnesians in this war.

Chapter 66

§1 In this same summer the Lacedaemonians and their allies made an expedition with a hundred ships against the island of Zacynthus, which lies over against Elis; its people are colonists of the Achaeans of the Peloponnese, and they were in alliance with the Athenians.

§2 There sailed with them a thousand hoplites of the Lacedaemonians, and Cnemos the Spartan as admiral. Landing on the land, they ravaged the greater part; and when the islanders would not yield, they sailed away home.

Chapter 67

§1 At the very end of the same summer, Aristeus the Corinthian, and the Lacedaemonian envoys Aneristus and Nicolaus and Protodamus and Timagoras the Tegean, and Pollis the Argive in his own person, journeying to Asia to the King — if perhaps they could persuade him to furnish money and to join in the war — came first to Sitalces son of Teres in Thrace, wishing to persuade him, if they could, to secede from the Athenian alliance and to march against Potidaea, where an army of the Athenians lay in siege, and thereafter to pass on their way through his land across the Hellespont to Pharnaces son of Pharnabazus, who was to send them up to the King.

§2 There chanced to be present with Sitalces envoys of the Athenians, Learchus son of Callimachus and Ameiniades son of Philemon; and these persuaded Sadocus son of Sitalces — him who had been made an Athenian citizen — to deliver the men into their hands, so that, crossing over to the King, they might not bring their share of harm to his city.

§3 He, persuaded, arrested them on their way through Thrace to the vessel in which they were to cross the Hellespont, before their going aboard — sending other men along with Learchus and Ameiniades, and ordering those men to hand the prisoners over; and these, taking them over, conveyed them to Athens.

§4 And when they had come to Athens, the Athenians, in fear of Aristeus — lest, having once escaped, he work them still further harm, since even before this he had appeared to manage all the business of Potidaea and of the Thracian side — put them all to death that very day, without trial, though there were things they wished to say; and they cast them into the ravine, justifying themselves as repaying the like of that with which the Lacedaemonians had made the beginning — killing the traders of the Athenians and of their allies whom they caught sailing about the Peloponnese in merchantmen, and casting them into ravines. For at the beginning of the war the Lacedaemonians destroyed, as enemies, all whom they took at sea, both those who were fighting along with the Athenians and those who stood with neither side.

Chapter 68

§1 At about the same time, as the summer was ending, the Ambraciots also marched — having raised themselves and many of the barbarians — against Argos the Amphilochian and the rest of Amphilochia.

§2 And from this the enmity of the Ambraciots towards the Argives first began.

§3 Argos the Amphilochian and the rest of Amphilochia were founded after the Trojan war by Amphilochus son of Amphiaraus — he, having come home and not being pleased with the state of things at Argos, founded the city in the Ambracian gulf, and named it Argos, the same name as his own fatherland.

§4 (and this city was the greatest of Amphilochia, and had the most powerful men for its inhabitants),

§5 Pressed by misfortunes many generations later, they brought in as joint settlers the Ambraciots, who were neighbours to the land of Amphilochia; and they were Hellenized in the tongue they now speak, then for the first time, by the Ambraciots who came to settle with them; the other Amphilochians are barbarians.

§6 In course of time the Ambraciots drove out the Argives and themselves held the city.

§7 The Amphilochians, when this had been done, put themselves into the hands of the Acarnanians, and both together called on the Athenians besides to their aid; and these sent them Phormio as general with thirty ships; and on Phormio’s arrival they took Argos by force and made slaves of the Ambraciots, and Amphilochians and Acarnanians founded the city afresh as a joint settlement.

§8 After this the alliance between the Athenians and the Acarnanians came into being for the first time.

§9 The Ambraciots — their hatred towards the Argives they had first conceived from the enslaving of themselves — later, in this war, made this expedition with their own men and the Chaonians and certain other barbarians of the neighbouring country; and marching upon Argos they held the countryside; but the city, when they had made their assault and could not take it, they let be, and they went back home and broke up, each according to its own nation. So much came to pass in this summer.

Chapter 69

§1 In the winter that followed, the Athenians sent twenty ships round the Peloponnese, and Phormio as general, who, cruising out of Naupactus, kept guard that none should sail out from Corinth and the Crisaean gulf, nor sail in; and they sent six other ships to Caria and Lycia, and with them Melesander as general, so that these might gather money in those parts, and so that they might not allow the pirates of the Peloponnesians, setting out from that base, to harm the passage of the merchantmen bound from Phaselis and Phoenicia and the mainland thence.

§2 And Melesander, going up into Lycia with a force of Athenians from the ships and of allies, died, and, having been defeated in battle, destroyed part of the army besides.

Chapter 70

§1 In this same winter the Potidaeans, since they were no longer able to hold out under the siege — and the invasions of the Peloponnesians into Attica were calling the Athenians off no more than before, and the grain was spent, and many other things besides had by now come upon them there concerning the food they must have, so that some of them had even tasted of one another — brought words to the Athenian generals set over them about a coming to terms, to Xenophon son of Euripides, and Hestiodorus son of Aristocleides, and Phanomachus son of Callimachus.

§2 And the generals accepted the overtures, seeing the distress of their army in its winter position, and that the city had by now spent two thousand talents upon the siege.

§3 On these terms they came to an agreement: that they should go out of the city — themselves and their children and their women and the auxiliaries — with one garment apiece, the women with two, and carrying a fixed sum of money for the road.

§4 And they went out under truce, to Chalcidice, and wherever each was able; and the Athenians found fault with their generals for having come to terms without them (for they supposed they could then have made themselves masters of the city on whatever terms they pleased), and later they sent out settlers of their own to Potidaea and settled them there. These things came to pass in this winter, and the second year of this war, which Thucydides wrote, was brought to its end.
