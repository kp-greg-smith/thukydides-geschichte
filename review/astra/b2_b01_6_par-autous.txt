Thucydides 2.2.4, transmitted Oxford/Jones text (inside the parenthesis): "(καὶ ἀνεῖπεν ὁ κῆρυξ, εἴ τις βούλεται κατὰ τὰ πάτρια τῶν πάντων Βοιωτῶν ξυμμαχεῖν, τίθεσθαι παρ᾽ αὑτοὺς τὰ ὅπλα)". Context: Thebans hold the Plataean market-place at night and invite Plataeans to join the alliance of all Boeotians.
Three current translations construe τίθεσθαι παρ᾽ αὑτοὺς τὰ ὅπλα as "lay down his arms at his own side" (each man beside himself).
Objection raised in review: transmitted αὑτούς is PLURAL reflexive while the subject τις is singular, so "beside himself" misreads; the sense should be "lay down his arms beside THEM", i.e. at the side of the Thebans present, as the gesture of adhesion.
Questions: (a) What does the transmitted παρ᾽ αὑτοὺς most plausibly mean here — reflexive referring to τις despite the number mismatch, or pointing to the Thebans? Cite the classical support (LSJ, scholarly or public-domain witnesses) for whichever reading you adopt. (b) Should a faithful modern translation print "at his own side", "beside them", or keep the openness with a brief reader note? Answer briefly.

**(a) “Beside the Thebans” is the more plausible reading.** Retaining αὑτούς, I would understand it as an **indirect reflexive**, looking back to the Thebans whose invitation the herald delivers—effectively “beside us” from their perspective. A Greek reflexive need not refer to the infinitive’s own subject: see [Smyth §§1225–26](https://grammars.alpheios.net/smyth/xhtml/body.1_div1.4_div2.17.html).

The strongest support is contextual and scholarly:

- [Gomme, commentary ad 2.2.4](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~simon/Athens/Thucydides3/awgomme3.pdf), explains “take his place with them,” predominantly “take sides with them.”
- [Crawley](https://classics.mit.edu/Thucydides/pelopwar.2.second.html) translates “ground arms with them.”
- [LSJ, τίθημι A.II.10(a–b)](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn:cite2:scaife-viewer:dictionaries.v1:lsj-n103922/), documents resting arms/taking military position and bearing arms for a side; surrender is a separate sense. This supports the idiom, though it does not independently settle the pronoun’s antecedent.

The reviewer’s **number argument alone is too categorical**: Greek can move from generalizing τις to a plural reference; see [Harris, *CQ* 43 (1993), p. 93 and n. 69](https://electronicsandbooks.com/edt/manual/Magazine/C/Classical%20Quarterly%20UK/1993/Harris%20-%201993%20-%20Apotimema%20Athenian%20Terminology%20for%20Real%20Security%20.pdf). That possibility does not make “his own side” preferable here.

**(b)** I would print **“place his arms beside them”**, with the brief note: “Beside the Thebans, signifying that he joined their side.” The evidence warrants that choice; there is no need to present both interpretations as equally likely.
